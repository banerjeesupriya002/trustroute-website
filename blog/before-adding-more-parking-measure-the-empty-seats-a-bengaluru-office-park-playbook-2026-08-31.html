Before adding more parking, measure the empty seats: a Bengaluru office-park playbook

A full parking garage does not automatically mean an office needs more parking. For Bengaluru employers, the more useful first step is to measure peak demand, vehicle occupancy and employees’ real commute constraints—then test voluntary options that move more people without penalising those who need to travel alone.

A full parking garage can make an office-park problem look simple: build another deck, lease an overflow lot or add another shuttle loop. But before committing capital to vehicle storage, employers should establish what is actually happening at the gate.

How many cars arrive during the busiest 30 minutes? How many bays are occupied at the peak, rather than merely assigned on paper? And, crucially, how many cars carry one person?

These questions matter in Bengaluru because the cost of road delay is already substantial. TomTom’s 2025 city data records an average travel time of 36 minutes and 9 seconds for a 10 km drive; its evening-rush estimate is 45 minutes and 27 seconds. The index estimates 168 hours of rush-hour time lost across the year. Those figures are city-level traffic indicators, not a forecast for any one employee or office campus, but they show why a few avoidable vehicle trips at the same peak can matter. More parking can reduce the scramble inside a campus. It cannot add capacity to the approach roads outside it. That is why facilities decisions should be paired with a people-movement plan, rather than treated only as a real-estate calculation.

Parking demand is not the same as commute demand

The Economic Survey 2025–26 argues that urban roads are too often used to store low-occupancy vehicles instead of moving people, and calls for a transport system led by higher-capacity public transport, with safe first- and last-mile connections and better demand management. An office park cannot repair Bengaluru’s citywide transport network, but it can avoid policies that make the single-occupant car the easiest default for every employee. That does not mean treating solo driving as a failure. Employees may need to drive alone because of disability or accessibility needs, caregiving, late shifts, unreliable last-mile options, safety preferences, changing work hours or the need to make several trips in a day. Women in particular should not be expected to compromise their comfort or judgement to meet a workplace mobility target.

The practical aim is narrower: make space-efficient choices easier for people who already have compatible routes and schedules, while preserving a fair fallback for people who do not.

Run a short baseline before changing the rules

A two- to four-week measurement exercise is usually more useful than launching a carpool campaign immediately. It should focus on patterns, not surveillance.

  • Measure peak demand: record occupied bays and vehicle entries in short morning and evening intervals. A site with 95% occupancy all day has a different problem from one that is full only from 9:15 to 10:00 a.m.
  • Use a voluntary commute survey: ask for a broad origin zone or PIN-code cluster, usual arrival and departure window, primary mode, and whether the employee would consider sharing part of a trip. Avoid collecting home addresses when a neighbourhood-level pattern is enough.
  • Ask about constraints explicitly: include accessibility needs, late-shift travel, childcare, variable office days, women-only matching preferences and Metro or bus connectivity. A programme designed only for a predictable 9-to-6 schedule will exclude many of the people it claims to serve.
  • Separate attendance patterns: employees attending Tuesday through Thursday may have more viable matches than rotating teams. Analyse them separately rather than averaging them into one misleading campus number.

The output should be a practical map of overlap: the arrival windows, broad residential clusters and days where a voluntary shared ride, Metro feeder option, staff bus or shifted start time could realistically work. It should not be a list of employees to pressure into changing how they travel.

Test whether occupancy—not supply—is the constraint

Once a baseline exists, an employer can model a small pilot honestly. For example, if 600 employees currently drive alone and 60 of them instead ride in cars that are already travelling to campus with spare seats, the site would receive 60 fewer cars that day. That is a 10% reduction in those solo-car arrivals and potentially 60 parking bays released at the peak.

It is only an illustration, not a forecast. It assumes that the 60 people would otherwise have driven, that suitable seats and schedules exist, and that participants continue to find the arrangement worthwhile. Those assumptions are precisely what a site-specific pilot must test.

This distinction prevents a common mistake: claiming congestion reduction from sign-ups. The meaningful measures are repeat shared trips, fewer vehicle entries during the peak, released bays and whether participants say the arrangement is reliable enough to continue.

Make parking policy fair before making it ambitious

Parking is a scarce workplace benefit. If an employer wants to encourage higher occupancy, its rules should be visible, proportionate and designed around exceptions.

  • Protect essential access first. Accessible bays, emergency and on-call requirements, and genuine late-shift needs should not be displaced by a carpool incentive.
  • Offer modest shared-ride priority. Convenient bays for cars arriving with two or more occupants can be a useful nudge, provided the verification method is not intrusive and employees can still opt out.
  • Use predictable allocation for the rest. Rotation, transparent permits or clearly communicated pricing are generally fairer than informal first-come, first-served arrangements.
  • Keep a no-drama fallback. A participant should be able to drive alone when a carpool falls through, a child is unwell or work runs late. A rigid policy turns a voluntary programme into a source of commute risk.

Employers should also avoid rewarding a one-time check-in more than reliable behaviour. A small recurring parking credit or priority access for regular shared arrivals is more aligned with the operational goal than a launch-week contest.

Build for choice, trust and difficult days

Matching is not merely a route problem. People need enough information to decide whether a recurring arrangement suits them, a simple way to coordinate changes and a credible option to decline. For women, this includes the ability to choose women-only matching where available, without requiring anyone to disclose more personal information than is necessary.

Verification, driver and vehicle-document checks, trip sharing, clear cancellation norms and reliability information can reduce uncertainty. They are risk-management tools—not a certification that any ride is safe, nor a substitute for an employee’s own judgement. Employers should publish clear reporting and escalation routes, especially for early-morning and late-evening travel, and should never describe a carpool programme as guaranteeing safety.

Part-trip matching can also be more realistic than a door-to-door carpool. A lift to a Metro station, or from a station to an office cluster, may fit more employees’ routes while avoiding a long detour for the vehicle owner.

Where TrustRoute fits—and where it does not

TrustRoute should be considered a possible coordination tool, not the mobility strategy itself. Its public site currently describes the product as coming soon; employers should confirm current availability, product controls and data practices before presenting it to employees as an operational service. The site describes intended features including identity verification, driver-license and vehicle-RC checks for car owners, women-only ride filtering, live trip sharing, reliability information and direct UPI payment between participants. If those features are available and suitable for the employer’s programme, TrustRoute may help willing colleagues find and coordinate compatible shared commutes. It cannot ensure attendance, eliminate traffic, guarantee the conduct or driving of participants, or replace safe staff transport where an employer determines that dedicated transport is needed.

Review the pilot like an operations decision

After eight to twelve weeks, compare like-for-like office days and review:

  • vehicle entries and occupied bays during the peak;
  • self-reported or voluntarily recorded shared-car arrivals;
  • repeat participation and cancellations;
  • employee feedback on reliability, privacy, comfort and fairness; and
  • which groups were not served by the programme.

The last measure is as important as the first. If employees in a particular corridor cannot form workable carpools, the answer may be a feeder shuttle, a revised office-day pattern, a later meeting schedule or better information about public transport. The evidence should determine the intervention.

The takeaway: Bengaluru’s congestion needs city-scale investment in transit, walking, cycling and demand management. At the office-park level, employers can still make a useful contribution: measure peak parking demand and empty seats before building more storage; protect employee choice; and test voluntary shared-commute options against real operational outcomes.
