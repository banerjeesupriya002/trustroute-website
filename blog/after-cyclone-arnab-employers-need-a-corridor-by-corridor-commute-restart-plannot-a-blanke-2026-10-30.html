After Cyclone Arnab, employers need a corridor-by-corridor commute restart plan—not a blanket return-to-office message

A reopened office does not prove that employees can safely complete the trip to it. After Cyclone Arnab, employers in Andhra Pradesh and Odisha should phase attendance using current official route information, workable last-mile access and individual constraints—then consider voluntary carpooling only on cleared routes.

An office can reopen before a commute does.

That is the central operational problem for employers responding to Cyclone Arnab. On October 28, 2026, Arnab crossed the Andhra Pradesh and Yanam coast between Bapatla and Visakhapatnam as a very severe cyclonic storm. The India Meteorological Department reported sustained winds of 125–135 kmph, gusting to 150 kmph, during landfall. By October 30, Arnab had weakened into a low-pressure area over north Chhattisgarh and adjoining east Madhya Pradesh—but a weaker weather system is not the same thing as a fully restored travel network.

Andhra Pradesh reported damage to homes, roads and electricity infrastructure, while the state’s highway response documented waterlogging on parts of NH-16 and NH-516C, including service roads and underpasses. NHAI also reported fallen trees, temporary traffic interruptions and pothole repairs across affected stretches. For an employee, the deciding problem may therefore be neither the office building nor the main highway. It may be the flooded lane before the highway, a dark walk from a bus stop, disrupted local transport or a household dealing with power loss.

The workplace lesson is straightforward: reopening a site is not the same as restarting every employee’s commute.

Why a blanket return-to-office instruction is risky

“Operations resume tomorrow” treats recovery as uniform. It is not. A main road may be open while feeder roads remain waterlogged. A bus service may restart while the stop is inaccessible. A journey that was reasonable in daylight may become unsuitable after an evening shift when lighting, mobile coverage or local transport is unreliable.

This matters especially for employees who have limited flexibility: shift workers, women travelling early or late, people with caregiving responsibilities, and staff whose homes or regular routes are in low-lying, rural or coastal areas. A compulsory attendance message can transfer the unresolved risk of recovery from the organisation to the individual commuter.

Public agencies must rightly prioritise life safety, rescue, relief and restoration of essential services. Before Arnab’s landfall, the National Crisis Management Committee said the objective was to minimise loss of life and damage, with essential services restored as quickly as possible. Odisha’s government similarly prioritised restoration of electricity, drinking water and communications. Employers should respect that sequence rather than assume that a partial restoration of road access means normal commuting can resume.

Use a corridor decision, not a citywide assumption

For the first few days after a cyclone, attendance decisions should be based on three tests: is the work genuinely needed on site; is the full route currently workable; and does the employee have a personal constraint that makes travel unreasonable?

  1. Group employees by broad travel corridors. Do not ask only whether an employee’s city is “open.” Identify the routes people actually use—for example, Kakinada–Rajahmundry, Visakhapatnam–Anakapalli or Berhampur–Gopalpur—and assess the door-to-door journey.
  2. Use official information first. Check district administration, traffic police, road-authority, railway and local public-transport updates. Employee reports can identify a problem early, but a forwarded message or a single driver’s account should not be used to compel travel.
  3. Test the last mile separately. Confirm more than the highway or rail line. Ask whether approach roads, pickup points, street lighting, pedestrian access and local connectivity are functioning.
  4. Apply a simple green–amber–red framework. Green corridors may support voluntary or essential attendance. Amber corridors need flexibility, later arrival windows and a no-penalty fallback. Red corridors should default to remote work, paid emergency leave or alternative duties where possible.

The aim is not to create a perfect live map. It is to prevent a broad corporate announcement from overriding a specific, known local hazard.

Phase attendance around critical work

A safer restart is rarely all-or-nothing. Begin with functions that must be on site and employees who have independently confirmed that their routes are workable. Other teams can remain remote, work staggered hours or defer non-essential on-site activity.

Managers need one explicit instruction: attendance is not a test of commitment. An employee should be able to say that their route, home situation, caregiving responsibilities or pickup point is unsafe without being pushed to reveal unnecessary personal detail. That approach is fairer, but it also gives facilities and HR teams better information about where the recovery plan is failing.

What the first employee message should answer

A useful cyclone-recovery update should answer practical questions rather than simply announce that the office is open:

  • Who is expected on site? Limit this initially to clearly identified critical roles.
  • Which routes have been assessed? Link to the relevant official advisories and state when the assessment was made.
  • What happens if conditions change during the trip? Allow late arrival, turn-backs and remote work without an attendance penalty.
  • How will late shifts end safely? Do not assume normal public transport, lighting or connectivity has returned simply because daytime traffic is moving.
  • Who can decide exceptions quickly? Give employees a reachable manager, HR contact or transport desk—not a generic mailbox.

It is sensible to appoint one temporary mobility lead for the recovery period. This person does not certify roads or replace government directions. Their job is to consolidate official updates, identify affected employee corridors, coordinate with security and facilities, and make sure the company’s message does not outrun conditions on the ground.

Carpooling belongs after route clearance—not before it

On a route that authorities indicate is usable, voluntary carpooling can help employees coordinate scarce seats and avoid isolated, costly trips while regular buses or shared autos are disrupted. It can be useful for colleagues with similar origins, compatible shifts and a safe agreed pickup point.

But it is not an emergency workaround. No carpool should be used to bypass a police closure, cross an inundated road, use a damaged bridge or disregard an evacuation or no-travel instruction. If official advice is not to travel, the employer response should be remote work, leave or a change in duties—not a better ride match.

For cleared, voluntary journeys, a structured platform can be more transparent than an informal chat group. A better restart message

After a cyclone, the responsible message is not “normal operations resume.” It is: “We are reopening in phases. Do not travel unless your role is needed on site and your full route, including the last mile, is workable. Follow local authority directions first. If your corridor or household situation makes travel unsafe, tell your manager or HR contact; there will be no attendance penalty.”

That is not a softer version of operations. It is better operational control. Roads, neighbourhood access, electricity, mobile networks and public transport recover on different timelines. Employers that manage the commute corridor by corridor will make fewer assumptions, put less pressure on affected employees and restore on-site work more reliably.
