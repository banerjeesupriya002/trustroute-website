When a 5-km Bengaluru trip reportedly takes two hours, offices should manage demand—not blame commuters

Reports of school buses taking more than two hours for short trips on Bengaluru’s Varthur–Gunjur corridor show how a local road failure can spill into office attendance, shift planning and family care. Employers cannot repair SH-35, but they can avoid adding unnecessary peak-hour demand.

On September 17, parents in Bengaluru’s Varthur–Gunjur belt reported school buses taking two hours or more to cover very short distances; some accounts described a five-kilometre trip taking more than two hours. The reports followed severe congestion on and around SH-35, not a normal benchmark for the corridor. Still, they illustrate how quickly a road disruption can consume a working day for families, drivers and employees travelling through the same bottlenecks.

Traffic police told Moneycontrol that rain-damaged road conditions and the closure of Gunjur Club Road for white-topping work had diverted more traffic towards SH-35. Parents sought pothole repairs, management of construction and heavy-vehicle traffic, and coordination among road agencies, traffic police and schools. On September 20, the Bengaluru East City Corporation commissioner directed immediate pothole repairs at Gunjur and Varthur junctions, including attention to damaged culvert slabs and school-bus movement.

Road agencies are responsible for fixing the road. But employers whose teams use the corridor have a separate, practical responsibility: avoid making a known disruption worse through rigid attendance rules or unnecessarily synchronised office arrivals.

Why an office policy can add to a local traffic problem

A fixed reporting time concentrates trips. When many employees are asked to reach an office at 9 or 9.30 am, a workplace adds demand to the same narrow time window as school drop-offs, buses, delivery vehicles and construction activity. A strict late-marking policy can also push people to leave far earlier than needed—or to drive when a later start, remote start or different in-office day would have worked better.

That does not mean every company should declare work from home whenever traffic is slow. It means the response should match the disruption. If a substantial share of an office is exposed to one corridor, employers should treat access as an operational issue: identify the affected group, temporarily spread arrival times, and protect employees from attendance penalties they cannot reasonably control.

This matters particularly in Bengaluru’s eastern growth corridors, where a short distance can involve several junctions, incomplete works and limited alternatives. It also matters for employees managing school pick-up, care duties or late shifts, for whom a two-hour delay is not merely an inconvenience.

A measured response for employers

1. Identify exposure without collecting more personal data than needed

Use an opt-in pulse survey for the affected office. Ask for broad origin area, usual approach corridor, typical arrival window, work shift and travel mode—not a precise home address. The aim is to understand whether a meaningful cluster of employees uses Varthur, Gunjur, Balagere, Panathur, Whitefield or another exposed approach.

A simple weekly view is enough: how many people report material delays, which arrival periods are most affected, and whether the disruption is continuing. That is more actionable than a generic message telling everyone to “plan ahead.”

2. Spread arrivals rather than simply asking people to leave earlier

For a defined period, replace one hard reporting time with arrival bands where the work permits it. Teams without a customer-facing or site-critical requirement may be able to begin remotely and arrive later, or choose an earlier or later band. Organisations can also avoid concentrating compulsory in-office days on a single weekday.

The point is not to shift congestion into an earlier hour. It is to reduce avoidable simultaneous demand. Managers should assess whether work is completed and teams are available when needed, rather than treating a few minutes at the office gate as the sole measure of performance.

3. Make the exception clear before people are stranded

Flexibility is less useful when an employee must bargain for it while stuck in traffic. Publish a temporary trigger—for example, a formally announced road closure, a confirmed civic-work diversion or repeated reports of exceptional travel times—and state what changes: no late-arrival penalty, permission to begin remotely, and an escalation route for teams that must maintain coverage.

This should be time-bound and reviewed. A temporary corridor protocol is not a substitute for a fair, durable hybrid-work or attendance policy.

4. Plan separately for school and late-shift constraints

Local reporting has highlighted the concentration of schools along the Varthur–Gunjur stretch and calls for better traffic management around school movements. Employers should avoid scheduling non-essential mandatory arrivals during predictable school peaks where possible. This is a small scheduling decision that may reduce stress for employees who are also parents or caregivers.

Late-shift staff need different safeguards: confirm departures early, avoid expecting people to wait alone at isolated pick-up points, and give them a clear contact if an arranged ride fails. These measures are especially relevant for women and others who may have legitimate safety concerns after dark; flexibility must not become pressure to accept a travel arrangement they do not want.

5. Offer carpooling as an option, not an attendance workaround

Voluntary carpooling will not repair a pothole, clear a diversion or guarantee an on-time arrival. It can, however, reduce the number of separate vehicle trips when colleagues who would otherwise drive alone choose to travel together. Participation should never be compulsory, and nobody should be expected to share a ride simply to meet an office mandate.

For employers that choose to support it, the useful role is coordination: communicate participation rules, allow reasonable pick-up buffers, identify appropriate meeting points, and keep personal-location data collection proportionate. A verified carpool platform such as TrustRoute can be one way to support voluntary matching among colleagues, provided the employer checks that the available verification, privacy and safety settings meet its own policy requirements. It should be presented as one commute option—not as a safety guarantee or a replacement for public road management.

Track whether the policy is helping

During a disruption, three measures are usually enough: the number of employees reporting material corridor delays, the operational impact on shifts or meetings, and voluntary changes in timing, mode or shared travel. Add anonymous feedback on whether anyone felt pressured to travel despite unreasonable conditions.

That turns a recurring complaint into a decision process. If repairs and traffic management improve conditions, arrival bands can end. If another closure begins, the company has a tested response ready. And if one office repeatedly relies on a fragile approach road, that evidence should inform future site, parking, shuttle and hybrid-work decisions.

The takeaway

The Varthur–Gunjur disruption in mid-September 2026 was linked to poor road conditions, ongoing works and diverted traffic. It is a reminder that commute reliability is shaped by more than the distance between home and office.

Employers cannot take over the job of road agencies. They can avoid amplifying a bottleneck by spreading non-essential peak-hour trips, applying humane attendance rules and offering voluntary support to employees with different travel and care constraints. That is not a perk; it is a more resilient way to run an office when a critical access corridor fails.
