A Metro-adjacent office is not automatically a commute-ready office

A nearby station can improve an office location, but it does not solve the full employee journey. Before signing a lease—or redesigning access at an existing site—employers should test the trip by shift, origin corridor and final-mile conditions.

“Near Metro” has become a shorthand for a good office location. It is a useful starting point, but it is not a commute plan.

CBRE’s India Office Occupier Survey 2026 found that 70% of surveyed occupiers prioritise commute infrastructure when selecting office locations. That makes connectivity a leasing consideration, not merely an employee perk. But the survey finding also exposes a common gap: a building can look connected on a brochure while the actual home-to-desk journey remains unreliable, expensive or uncomfortable for the people expected to use it.

For Indian employers, the practical question is therefore not simply, How far is the nearest Metro station? It is, Which employees can complete the journey reliably at their actual arrival and departure times?

Distance is only one part of accessibility

A station two kilometres from an office may work well for an employee arriving at 10 am in dry weather. It may work poorly for someone finishing a late shift, travelling with a laptop, managing a childcare pickup, or facing an unlit walk and an uncertain feeder connection. The same applies to a road-facing office: easy car access for some employees can coexist with long gate queues, scarce parking and difficult pedestrian access for others.

This is why office-location analysis should treat commuting as a chain rather than a pin on a map: home area, first-mile access, main transit leg, interchange, station exit, final kilometre and campus entry. A failure at any point can make the whole option impractical.

The issue is especially relevant in large Indian employment clusters, where Metro expansion can create substantial new access but does not automatically resolve last-mile travel. In Bengaluru’s Electronic City, WRI India and the Toyota Mobility Foundation worked with eight companies on a programme using behavioural interventions, digital tools, incentives and employer engagement to encourage public-transport use. According to the foundation, the initiative supported more than 1,800 employees in changing travel behaviour and facilitated more than 120,000 Metro trips. The important lesson is not that a single programme can be copied everywhere; it is that employers can help turn transit infrastructure into a workable daily option.

Why employers have a role without becoming transport operators

Real-estate teams choose the site. Facilities teams manage gates and parking. HR teams hear the employee-experience concerns. Administration may manage transport vendors. Yet the commute often has no clear owner.

That fragmentation produces avoidable problems: an underused station connection because nobody has explained the final leg; solo driving because employees on the same corridor cannot easily coordinate; or a late-shift gap that is discovered only after people have started using the new office.

Employers cannot repair a city’s footpaths or run its public transport system. They can, however, make better decisions about the access conditions they control: shift timing, gate operations, pickup areas, parking rules, commute information and voluntary support for shared trips. This is consistent with travel-demand management, which combines better public transport and streets with measures such as parking management to reduce pressure from private-vehicle travel.

A practical commute-readiness checklist

1. Start with employee origins and shifts

Before a lease is signed—or before a major return-to-office change—run a voluntary, privacy-conscious commute survey. Use broad home zones or PIN-code clusters rather than exact residential addresses. Ask about usual arrival and departure windows, current mode, major barriers and the parts of the journey employees consider unreliable.

Map the results by corridor and shift. An average commute time across the workforce is less useful than knowing which corridors lack a credible transit connection, which employees leave after dark and where parking demand is concentrated.

2. Test the journey at the time people actually travel

Site visits should cover the route from station exit or pickup point to the office gate, not just the lobby and parking basement. Check walkability, shade or rain cover where possible, crossings, lighting, signage, waiting space, feeder availability and the time required to clear security. For offices with evening or night shifts, test the relevant departure period rather than assuming the morning journey represents the whole day.

This is also where commuter safety becomes operationally relevant. A short final leg is not necessarily a usable one if the route is isolated, poorly lit or lacks a clear pickup point. Employers should identify the gap and choose the response that fits it—such as improved access arrangements with the property manager, a timed feeder, a designated pickup zone or staggered exits.

3. Make public transport the backbone where it works

Metro and bus services can move far more people than a larger parking garage. Where public transport is a realistic option, employers can make it easier to use by sharing route guidance, publishing gate-to-station instructions, aligning flexible arrival windows with service patterns and ensuring the final leg is understandable.

WRI India’s Bengaluru work identifies last-mile cost and waiting time as barriers to Metro use, including for higher-income commuters who have private-vehicle alternatives. That is a useful caution against generic “use public transport” campaigns: information alone will not solve a weak connection.

4. Use voluntary shared rides for the remaining gaps

Carpools can be useful for repeated origin-and-shift patterns, dispersed residential clusters, early starts, late finishes and station-to-campus gaps that transit does not serve well. They should remain voluntary, with clear cost arrangements and a practical alternative for employees who cannot or do not wish to participate.

An employer’s role is coordination, not compulsion: identify broad compatible corridors, provide safe pickup and drop-off points, and set clear conduct and escalation expectations. If an organisation considers a platform such as TrustRoute, it should verify the product’s current identity, matching, payment, privacy and support features before deployment, and ensure that its use fits the company’s transport, data-handling and safety policies. No carpool tool removes all risk or substitutes for emergency processes and personal judgement.

5. Treat parking data as evidence

Parking is finite infrastructure, not a neutral entitlement. Track occupancy by time, entry queues, overflow, visitor demand and vehicle occupancy where practical. A full parking lot does not by itself prove that more parking is the answer; it may show that employees lack credible alternatives.

Only after alternatives are workable should employers consider measures such as priority spaces for carpools, accessibility needs and essential late-shift roles. The goal is reliable access for more people, not simply a different rule for the same bottleneck.

6. Measure whether the journey improved

Registration counts and launch emails are not outcomes. Review mode share, average vehicle occupancy, parking utilisation, uptake by corridor, repeat use of any shared-ride arrangement, and employee feedback on predictability and the final leg. A low-use option may reflect poor timing or an unsafe pickup point rather than a lack of interest.

Put commute readiness into the lease decision

For a prospective office, the pre-lease checklist should ask: What share of the workforce has a realistic transit option? Does station access work during the first and last shifts? Is the walk usable during monsoon conditions? Which corridors will still depend on shared mobility or employer-arranged transport? What will happen at the gate when several hundred people arrive within the same hour?

The best-connected office is not necessarily the one with the most impressive connectivity diagram. It is the one whose workforce can complete the daily journey with reasonable reliability across different shifts and seasons.

The takeaway: commute infrastructure belongs in the office-location decision, but commute readiness requires ongoing operating decisions. Transit should be strengthened where it works; first- and last-mile failures should be identified rather than ignored; and voluntary shared rides can fill specific gaps without being presented as a universal solution. TrustRoute can be relevant as a potential coordination layer, but employers should evaluate it against real employee needs and their own policies—not treat any app as a replacement for sound access planning.
