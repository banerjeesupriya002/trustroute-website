A panic button is not a safety system: the lesson in Delhi’s bus-safety order

Delhi’s late-August bus-safety order requires working tracking devices and accessible panic buttons. Its more useful lesson for buses, office transport and carpools is that a safety feature works only when the trip, the people involved and the response process are clear.

A panic button can be installed, labelled and checked during an inspection. None of that, on its own, tells a passenger what will happen after they press it.

That is the important lesson in Delhi’s recent bus-safety directions. The order, reported on September 2 and 3, requires bus operators in Delhi—including operators of buses registered in other states—to keep vehicle-location tracking devices and panic buttons functional. It also calls for panic buttons to be accessible and properly maintained, along with serviceable first-aid kits and fire extinguishers. Other requirements address visibility, crew conduct and basic operating discipline: restrictions on curtains and unauthorised window films, secure night parking, verified crew and avoidance of unsafe or unauthorised stops. Violations may attract action under the law.

The requirements matter because they move the conversation beyond whether a bus has a gadget. They raise a harder operational question: can the operator identify the vehicle, understand the situation and act when a passenger needs help?

A safety device needs a response chain

India’s 2018 Vehicle Location Tracking Device and Emergency Button Order established a national framework for tracking devices and emergency buttons in specified public-service vehicles. The associated AIS-140 standard contemplates device-health information and emergency alerts reaching the relevant State or Union Territory emergency-response system. That is the difference between equipment and a system: the technology has to be connected to monitoring, decision-making and response.

For a panic alert to be useful in practice, several links need to hold:

  • the button must work and be reachable;
  • the vehicle’s identity and location must be available;
  • the alert must reach a monitored destination;
  • a named person or team must know what to do next; and
  • the incident must be recorded and reviewed afterwards.

If one link fails, the feature may provide less protection than passengers reasonably expect. The same principle applies to GPS that is not transmitting, a staff badge issued without meaningful verification, or a safety policy that does not identify who is responsible after normal office hours.

Why the lesson extends beyond buses

For many people in Delhi-NCR, the commute is a chain of journeys rather than one ride: a walk to the Metro, a feeder vehicle, an office shuttle, an auto, a cab or a shared car. The points of uncertainty often sit between those modes—at an unfamiliar pickup point, after a late meeting, or when a driver changes a route or timing.

This is particularly relevant to women commuters. A practical safety assessment is not limited to whether a vehicle displays a panic button. Before boarding, a rider should be able to establish who is operating the trip, what vehicle they are entering, where it is expected to go and how they can get help if the plan changes.

Consider the difference between an informal message saying, “Two seats towards Noida,” and a ride arrangement that records the vehicle, participants, pickup point, expected drop and fare before boarding. Neither can guarantee that a trip will be incident-free. But the second arrangement reduces ambiguity and leaves a clearer record if a rider needs to report a problem or seek assistance.

Five questions commuters can use

Whether the trip is by bus, employer-provided transport, cab or voluntary carpool, these questions are more revealing than a long list of feature names:

  1. Who is involved?
    Can the rider identify the driver or car owner and, where relevant, the fellow riders? A profile or badge is more useful when there is a real verification process behind it.
  2. What is the agreed trip?
    Before boarding, confirm the vehicle, pickup location, expected drop, departure time and broad route. Put material changes in writing in the app or group chat so that everyone sees the same information.
  3. Can a trusted person follow the journey if needed?
    Live-location sharing can give a friend or family member useful context during a trip. It is not a substitute for calling emergency services, and riders should decide carefully who receives that information.
  4. Who owns the response?
    A transport provider or employer should be able to name its after-hours contact and escalation path. In an immediate emergency in Delhi, call 112. Delhi Police also lists 1091 as its helpline for women in distress.
  5. What happens after a report?
    There should be a way to flag unsafe conduct, dangerous driving, repeated cancellations or route deviations—and a clear owner for reviewing the report. A reporting form without follow-up is not meaningful accountability.

What employers should check

Companies that arrange shuttles, cabs or commute programmes should treat these questions as part of duty of care, not merely transport administration. At minimum, the organisation should be able to retrieve the assigned vehicle and trip details, identify the responsible transport or security contact, and explain the escalation procedure for late shifts or disruptions.

That does not mean an employer should continuously monitor every employee. Good design uses clear opt-in, limited access to trip data, retention rules and transparent communication about who can see information and when. Privacy and safety are not opposites: the goal is to collect and share no more than is necessary while ensuring that authorised responders have useful information in an incident.

It also means avoiding safety advice that puts the whole burden on women—such as simply telling employees to share their location or avoid travelling alone. Those precautions can help, but organisational responsibility is broader: reliable trip information, accountable vendors, sensible pickup practices and a response plan that works after hours.

Where coordinated carpooling can help

Voluntary carpooling cannot replace safe public transport, enforcement or emergency services. It can, however, be safer to assess when it is coordinated rather than improvised through an open chat group.

TrustRoute’s public App Store description says that it uses Aadhaar OKYC and DigiLocker-based identity checks for members; asks car owners to submit driving-licence and vehicle-registration details; and offers trip tracking, a shareable tracking link, group trip chat, fixed per-stop fares and rider reliability information. Those are tools for reducing uncertainty before and during a ride—not guarantees of safety or substitutes for emergency help. Commuters should still check the trip details, use their judgement and call 112 in an immediate emergency.

The takeaway

Delhi’s bus-safety order is not chiefly a story about panic buttons. It is a reminder that commuter safety is operational: working equipment, visible trips, identifiable people, disciplined stops and parking, a live escalation route, and someone accountable for acting.

Before the next commute, ask a more useful question than “Does this ride have a panic button?” Ask: “If I need help, who can identify this trip, who can respond, and what information will they already have?”
