Office start times are transport policy: what new national commute data means for employers

India’s first National Household Travel Survey shows that fixed-workplace trips are heavily concentrated before 10 am. Employers cannot fix every road or train service, but they can reduce avoidable pressure by making arrival times, transit connections and voluntary carpooling work together.

An office start time is also a transport decision. When a workplace expects most people at a gate, desk or stand-up at the same hour, it adds demand to the same roads, stations, buses and last-mile links at the same time. That is not an argument for shifting every employee earlier. It is an argument for treating the timing of office work as something that can be designed.

India’s first National Household Travel Survey factsheet, released by the National Statistics Office on October 5, 2026, puts useful national numbers behind a familiar experience. Among workers aged 15 and above who made within-travel-day trips to a fixed workplace, 56.5% usually left between 8 am and 10 am. A further 33.7% left between 6 am and 8 am, and 4.7% before 6 am. In total, 94.9% left before 10 am.

The survey covered 492,023 households in 14,274 villages and 10,377 urban blocks between July 2025 and June 2026. It records trips of one kilometre or more, so it does not capture every short walk to a bus stop, parking area or office entrance. Nor does a national average diagnose one company’s commute problem. But it does show why a single default arrival time can create a predictable demand spike.

A short commute can still be a difficult one

The factsheet reports that 83.4% of workers travelling to a fixed workplace reached it in under 30 minutes; the average journey time was 25 minutes nationally and 27 minutes in urban areas. Urban workers reported average monthly commuting expenditure of ₹1,044, compared with ₹785 nationally.

Those figures should not be read as evidence that the commute is easy. A 25-minute average can conceal a trip with a fragile transfer: a delayed feeder bus, an unsafe walk from a station, a crowded parking exit or a pickup point that is difficult to find in rain. It also says little about workers whose long journeys fall into the minority, or about the time spent arranging childcare and household responsibilities around a rigid reporting hour.

The more useful conclusion for employers is modest: parking occupancy and late-arrival reports are not the whole story. They are often the visible result of a shared arrival peak, uneven access to transit and limited flexibility in how work is scheduled.

Do not turn “leave earlier” into the policy

Asking everyone to leave home earlier usually moves the pressure rather than reducing it. If several employers do the same, queues simply begin earlier. It can also shift costs onto people who have the least control over their journeys: parents coordinating school drop-offs, workers dependent on infrequent buses, employees with disabilities, and people travelling at hours when walking or waiting conditions are poor.

A better question is: which roles genuinely need the same start time, and which do not? Customer-facing, shift-based and site-dependent teams may require fixed coverage. Many desk-based teams do not require every employee to arrive within the same 30-minute window. The distinction matters because flexibility that exists only on paper will not change travel behaviour if managers still schedule mandatory in-person meetings at 9 am.

A practical employer checklist

1. Measure the journey without collecting more personal data than necessary. Use an opt-in survey alongside aggregated entry data. Ask employees for broad origin areas, usual arrival windows, main travel mode, office days, first- and last-mile difficulties, and constraints such as caregiving, disability access or shift work. Avoid requiring precise home addresses when clusters are sufficient for planning.

2. Offer defined arrival bands, then protect them. For teams whose work allows it, set workable bands rather than an informal promise of flexibility. For example, teams may choose among agreed windows with a shared collaboration period later in the morning. Managers should avoid defaulting to early mandatory meetings, and office teams should monitor whether every department selects the same band.

3. Plan around the full transit chain. The NHTS was designed in part to identify transport-access gaps and inform route, frequency and scheduling decisions. Employers can apply the same logic at site level: test the walk from station to office, the shuttle connection, waiting areas, lighting, rain cover and realistic transfer buffers. A Metro-adjacent address is useful only if the journey beyond the station also works.

4. Treat parking as a lever, not the objective. Parking data can reveal demand, but adding spaces does not resolve a synchronised arrival peak. Where appropriate, employers can reserve convenient spaces for carpools, prioritise accessible parking and make sure that any parking change does not disadvantage employees who must drive. The test is whether the policy creates a viable alternative, not whether it merely makes solo driving harder.

5. Test safety and fairness before changing hours. Consult women employees, late-shift teams and employees with accessibility or caregiving needs. An arrival policy should preserve the ability to opt out, name safe pickup and drop-off arrangements, and provide a clear route to report problems. A later or earlier shift is not automatically safer, cheaper or more practical.

Where voluntary carpooling can help

Public transport, walking and cycling should remain central where they are practical. Carpooling is most relevant for some of the car trips that remain: people travelling from compatible areas to the same workplace or transit node at similar times, especially where the final connection is weak. It should be voluntary, cost-sharing based and designed as a complement to—not a replacement for—reliable public transport.

Coordination is the hard part. A useful carpool arrangement lets participants review the route, pickup point, expected fare and relevant trust information before committing. Employers should not publish home addresses, compel participation or imply that a shared ride is guaranteed to be available.

The operating lesson

The national survey does not prescribe one office schedule for India. It does, however, make the morning concentration plain: most fixed-workplace commuters in its scope leave before 10 am, and more than half leave in the two hours from 8 am to 10 am.

For employers, the practical response is not a blanket instruction to beat traffic. Start with the actual journeys employees make. Then align arrival expectations, meeting norms, transit connections, safe access and voluntary shared rides around those realities. That can make the commute more manageable without assuming that every employee has the same route, risk or degree of choice.
