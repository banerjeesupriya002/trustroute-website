When monsoon disruption becomes a business-continuity issue: an employer playbook beyond WFH

Gurugram’s August 25 work-from-home advisory showed why heavy rain is not simply an attendance issue. Employers need pre-set decision triggers, clear communications, protection for late-shift staff and a mobility plan that treats carpooling as voluntary support—not a reason to travel in unsafe conditions.

A delayed arrival can look like an attendance issue—until waterlogged roads, stalled traffic and disrupted last-mile travel affect whole teams at once. At that point, the problem is operational: can essential work continue without asking people to take avoidable risks?

That distinction mattered in Gurugram on August 24, 2026. After heavy rain and widespread waterlogging, the district administration advised corporate offices to guide employees to work from home on Tuesday, August 25. The advisory was intended to reduce congestion and allow civic agencies to carry out road and drain repairs. District data cited by The Indian Express recorded 75 mm of rainfall in Gurugram tehsil between 8 am and 5 pm that day.

The lesson is not that every weather alert requires an office closure. It is that a weather-related work-from-home decision is a continuity measure, not a workplace perk. Organisations that decide only after employees have already begun difficult commutes are responding too late.

Why this belongs in a continuity plan

Monsoon disruption is uneven. An employee living near a Metro station may have a manageable journey, while another may depend on a flooded service road, a two-wheeler or a poorly lit late-night last mile. A blanket instruction to “use your judgement” can push the burden of a company-wide disruption onto the people with the least bargaining power or flexibility.

This matters because urban travel already consumes substantial time before rain is added to the equation. The Economic Survey 2025–26, citing the TomTom Traffic Index 2024, says commuters in Bengaluru, Mumbai and New Delhi city centres lost 117, 103 and 76 hours respectively in rush-hour congestion over a year. These are baseline congestion estimates, not measures of rain disruption; the useful inference for employers is that a short, intense downpour can overwhelm an already strained commute network.

A credible plan therefore starts with one principle: reduce non-essential travel before trying to optimise the travel that remains.

Use three operating modes, agreed before the monsoon

Rather than leaving each manager to improvise, employers can establish three plain-language modes.

1. Remote-first: Use this when a district authority issues a work-from-home or travel advisory, when access to the office is materially affected, or when conditions make travel unreasonable for a significant share of staff. Identify genuinely essential on-site functions in advance. “Essential” should mean the work cannot be performed remotely—not merely that in-person attendance is preferred.

2. Reduced-footfall: Use this when the office can function but major approach routes are unreliable. Keep only necessary teams on site, move meetings online, and offer broad arrival and departure windows. The objective is to lower demand at the same peak hour, not to preserve a normal attendance pattern.

3. On-site operations with flexibility: Use this only when building access and employee routes are reasonably manageable. Continue to allow remote participation, later arrival or early departure when local conditions worsen. A safe decision for one office corridor may not be a safe decision for every neighbourhood employees travel from.

Assign decision ownership in writing—typically to facilities, HR and the business-continuity lead—and name a backup. Inputs should include district administration notices, India Meteorological Department updates, building-access conditions and route reports from transport or facilities teams.

Weather colours are context, not an automatic office rule

The India Meteorological Department’s public warning framework describes yellow as “be updated,” orange as “be prepared” and red as “take action.” IMD’s warning SOP is useful for situational awareness, but a colour alone should not decide whether a particular office opens. Local flooding, official district instructions, transport disruption and the condition of the office approach road matter just as much.

Employers should instead publish escalation triggers employees can understand. Examples include:

  • a district administration advisory on remote work, closure or non-essential travel;
  • local waterlogging affecting common employee access routes or public transport connections;
  • flooding, power problems or unsafe access at the office building, parking area or basement;
  • material delays, diversions or suspension of company transport; and
  • late-shift travel that would leave employees dependent on poorly lit, flooded or infrequent transport links.

These are prompts for a decision, not a rigid formula. The value is consistency: employees should not have to make a case individually after a route has become unsafe.

Communicate before the commute starts

The most useful weather message is often sent the previous evening or early enough in the morning for people to change plans. A notice after employees have already left home may document a decision, but it does little to reduce exposure.

Every company-wide update should answer five questions:

  • Which operating mode applies today?
  • Who, if anyone, is expected on site?
  • What flexibility applies to arrival, departure and attendance?
  • Who can approve an exception or help with a route problem?
  • When will the next update be issued?

The central message should also state plainly that no employee is expected to take an unsafe route to meet an attendance expectation. Managers need the same clarity: they should not privately reverse the central guidance, demand photographs as proof of flooding or treat weather-related disruption as a performance failure.

Late shifts need a separate decision

Morning conditions are not a reliable guide to the journey home. Rain may intensify after dark, public transport frequency may fall, and a route that was passable at 6 pm may be unsuitable at 10 pm. Employers should decide early whether teams should leave before conditions deteriorate, work remotely where feasible, or use an approved alternative arrangement.

This is especially important for employees travelling alone, including women who may face a more difficult last mile after dark. Practical measures are better than broad assurances: early release, a staffed escalation contact, an employee-controlled check-in option and a clear right to decline a route or ride without having to justify the decision. No policy can guarantee personal safety; the responsible approach is to give employees time, choice and an alternative.

Where carpooling fits—and where it does not

On a day when the office is operating and routes are manageable, a voluntary shared ride can help some employees coordinate a predictable commute. It may be particularly useful where a public-transport connection is weak or the final leg to an office hub is difficult.

But carpooling is not a response to flooded roads, poor visibility, an official work-from-home advisory or a serious travel-safety concern. It should never be presented as a way to preserve office attendance during severe weather. The order of decisions matters: assess conditions, remove non-essential travel, and only then support the travel that remains.

That is the limited, practical place for a workplace carpool option such as TrustRoute. Employers should treat it as a voluntary coordination channel—not company transport, not an emergency service and not a substitute for remote work. Before including any third-party carpool service in a continuity plan, the employer should confirm its live availability, user-verification process, privacy controls, support arrangements and whether employees have a realistic non-carpool alternative.

Employers should also minimise data collection. They do not need to publish home addresses or make participation visible to managers. A pickup area can be agreed directly by participants, and choosing not to carpool must carry no attendance or career consequence.

A 30-minute response checklist

  • Before monsoon: define operating modes, decision owners, essential roles, communication templates and a late-shift protocol.
  • On a warning day: monitor IMD and district updates; check building access, company transport and common employee corridors.
  • Before commute time: issue one company-wide decision covering attendance, remote-work expectations, manager instructions and the next update time.
  • During the day: allow remote meeting access, staggered timing and early release as conditions change.
  • After the event: record route failures, building-access problems and communication gaps, then revise the playbook while the details are fresh.

The takeaway: Monsoon resilience is not about asking employees to become more determined commuters. It is about making early decisions that reduce unnecessary exposure while preserving essential work. A voluntary carpool option can support a manageable office day, but the safer choice in severe conditions is often the simplest one: do not ask people to travel.
