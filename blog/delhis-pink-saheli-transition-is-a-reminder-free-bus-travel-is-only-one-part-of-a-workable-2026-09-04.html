Delhi’s Pink Saheli transition is a reminder: free bus travel is only one part of a workable commute

Delhi has extended paper Pink Tickets until September 17, 2026 as it shifts eligible commuters to the Pink Saheli Smart Card. The transition protects access to free bus travel—but women’s mobility also depends on the walk, transfer and final leg around that bus or Metro ride.

Free bus travel can make a meaningful difference to a woman deciding whether a college, job, training programme or shift is realistically within reach. But the fare is only one part of that decision. The journey also has to feel workable before boarding and after alighting.

That is the practical lesson in Delhi’s transition from paper Pink Tickets to the Pink Saheli Smart Card. The Delhi Transport Corporation (DTC) lists an August 14, 2026 notice extending the deadline for mandatory use of the Pink Saheli card. Akashvani News subsequently reported that paper Pink Tickets for women’s free travel on DTC buses would continue through September 17, 2026, with the government describing the extension as a way to prevent disruption during the changeover. ([dtc.delhi.gov.in](https://dtc.delhi.gov.in/))

This is more than an administrative grace period. A fare-support scheme loses value if people are unable to use it while enrolment, card collection or familiarisation is still underway. Continuity matters. So does being precise about what the scheme solves: it reduces the price of travel on eligible DTC and Cluster bus trips; it does not, by itself, resolve a difficult walk to the stop, an uncomfortable interchange, or a sparse late-evening connection home.

Fare support is already operating at a significant scale

Delhi’s Economic Survey 2025–26 reports that, through February 2026, women made 56.21 crore free trips on DTC and Delhi government bus services, with ₹562.17 crore provided as subsidy during FY 2025–26. The survey also describes the move to a Pink Saheli Smart Card/National Common Mobility Card arrangement. ([delhiplanning.delhi.gov.in](https://delhiplanning.delhi.gov.in/sites/default/files/2026-03/economic_survey_english_0.pdf))

The implication is straightforward: recurring transport costs are material household costs, particularly for students, workers at the start of their careers, and people whose paid work is combined with caregiving. Removing a fare can expand practical choice. It should not, however, be treated as proof that the entire commute is accessible.

Safety concerns can carry their own time penalty. A World Bank working paper cites research involving roughly 3,800 Delhi University students in which women were estimated to be willing to travel 27 additional minutes per day—about 40% more than their daily travel time—for a route they perceived as safer. The figure is not a reason to normalise longer journeys for women. It is evidence that perceived safety can reshape route choices and access to opportunities. ([documents.worldbank.org](https://documents.worldbank.org/curated/en/099818202122438574/pdf/IDU1d307646a185e114fe31a5d5182d8e0dd0305.pdf?utm_source=openai))

The relevant unit is the whole trip, not the bus segment

A commute is usually a chain: the walk or feeder ride to a stop; the bus or Metro trunk segment; a transfer; and the last leg to an office, campus, neighbourhood or home. A free bus ride lowers the cost of one link. The other links can still determine whether someone chooses to travel.

Consider a commuter returning after an evening shift. If the final stop is some distance from home, the central question may not be the bus fare. It may be whether the route is well lit and active, whether a feeder is available, whether the pickup point is clear, or whether the traveller has a reliable plan if the connection is missed. These are ordinary operational details, but they are often where confidence in a commute is won or lost.

Delhi has taken steps within bus operations: the Economic Survey records 2,983 bus marshals deployed in DTC and Cluster buses as of February 28, 2026 for the safety and security of women passengers. That is relevant support on board. Yet an effective safety approach must also consider the street, stop and transfer environment around the vehicle. ([delhiplanning.delhi.gov.in](https://delhiplanning.delhi.gov.in/sites/default/files/2026-03/economic_survey_english_0.pdf))

What a better first- and last-mile plan looks like

There is no single answer for every corridor. On busy routes, the priority may be safer walking access, crossings, lighting and dependable feeder services. On repeat office or campus corridors—especially early-morning or late-evening ones—voluntary ride coordination may be useful as a complement to bus and Metro travel.

The important distinction is between a planned, transparent arrangement and an improvised one. Before accepting any shared ride, commuters should be able to answer basic questions: Who is travelling? Where exactly is the pickup? What is the expected cost? What happens if somebody is late? How will the group communicate?

A practical checklist for commuters and employers

  • Choose visible pickup and drop points: Prefer locations that are easy to identify, reasonably active and not isolated from the onward walking route.
  • Plan the missed-connection scenario: Know the next bus, Metro or feeder option, and keep a fallback contact or transport plan for late hours.
  • Share only what is necessary: Use trip details and live location thoughtfully; do not make home-address information more widely visible than the trip requires.
  • Look for transparent ride information: A shared trip is easier to assess when the people involved, pickup point, vehicle details and price are clear beforehand.
  • Keep participation voluntary: Neither employers nor colleagues should pressure anyone to carpool, particularly where a person is uncomfortable with the route, timing or group.
  • Design for late shifts: Employers should map common employee origins and destinations, provide clear escalation contacts, and avoid treating a shared ride as a substitute for a proper late-shift transport policy.
  • Offer preference-based options where possible: Women-only matching may matter to some commuters, but it should be a choice rather than an assumption about every woman’s needs.

For employers, the most useful first step is often modest: ask employees where the trip breaks down. Is it the walk to the station, the lack of a last-mile option after 8 pm, a confusing pickup area, or inconsistent shift-release times? The answer helps distinguish problems that require infrastructure or transport-provider action from those that can be eased through better coordination.

Where TrustRoute fits—and where it does not

For commuters with recurring first- or last-mile gaps, a platform such as TrustRoute can be considered as one voluntary coordination option. Its value should be judged against the same practical checklist: clear trip information, appropriate privacy controls, transparent costs, dependable communication and meaningful user choice. Commuters should review the features available to them and use their own judgement before joining any ride.

TrustRoute is not a replacement for reliable public transport, safe streets, well-designed interchanges or employer responsibility for late-working staff. Nor can any platform guarantee a risk-free trip. The more realistic role is narrower: helping regular travellers coordinate a known part of a commute when sharing is suitable for them.

The takeaway: Delhi’s extension of paper Pink Tickets through September 17, 2026 is a sensible continuity measure during the Pink Saheli rollout. The larger mobility test is whether women can complete the whole trip—at a reasonable cost, with predictable connections and without having to absorb a safety-related detour as the price of participation.
