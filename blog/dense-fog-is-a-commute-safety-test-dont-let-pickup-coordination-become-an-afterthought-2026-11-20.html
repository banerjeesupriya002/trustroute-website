Dense fog is a commute-safety test: don’t let pickup coordination become an afterthought

IMD has forecast dense fog for Haryana, Chandigarh and Delhi, and Punjab, through November 22. For office commuters, the response is not merely slower driving: it is flexible attendance and a clear pickup protocol that makes delays, changes and opting out safer.

A dense-fog morning can unravel an ordinary office pickup before the vehicle reaches the main road. A driver may miss a service-lane turn; a colleague may be waiting on the wrong side of a poorly visible road; or a late change to the car or pickup point may leave people trying to identify one another beside moving traffic.

On November 20, 2026, the India Meteorological Department (IMD) issued dense-fog warnings for Haryana, Chandigarh and Delhi, and for Punjab, for November 20–22. These are subdivision-level forecasts, not a promise that every neighbourhood will have identical visibility at the same time. That is precisely why employers and carpool groups should leave room to delay, alter or cancel a trip rather than treating the usual schedule as fixed. ([mausam.imd.gov.in](https://mausam.imd.gov.in/imd_latest/contents/subdivisionwise-warning_mc.php?id=17))

Fog changes the commute plan—not just the driving speed

Reduced visibility is first a driving risk. NHAI’s published fog guidance for National Highway users calls for lane discipline, a safe distance from the vehicle ahead and a speed low enough to stop within the visible distance. It advises driving at 30 km/h or below in dense fog, and says vehicles should not be parked on a highway except at designated locations. That guidance is especially relevant to highway sections; it should not be misread as a licence to drive at 30 km/h on every urban street regardless of local conditions, signs or traffic rules. ([pib.gov.in](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2204143&lang=1®=1))

For office carpools, there is a second problem: coordination. An unclear pickup instruction can lead to an unnecessary road crossing, a sudden U-turn, or a passenger waiting at a kerb where drivers cannot see them early enough. Those are avoidable decisions, not inevitable consequences of winter weather.

The practical question on a foggy morning is not, “How do we preserve the normal plan?” It is, “What is the safest workable version of this journey today?”

Use a short, pre-agreed fog protocol

Employers do not need to write a lengthy weather policy for every carpool. They do need to remove the pressure and ambiguity that make people improvise in poor conditions. A workable protocol can be agreed the evening before and reconfirmed in the morning.

  • Make attendance flexibility explicit. Where work permits, allow a later start, remote work or a delayed journey after visibility improves. A general instruction to “be on time” can unintentionally reward risk-taking.
  • Use one fixed, recognisable pickup point. Prefer a residential or office gate, a marked parking area or another location away from fast-moving traffic. “Near the flyover” is not a useful instruction in low visibility. Do not ask a rider to cross a high-speed road or wait on a highway shoulder.
  • Confirm twice. Confirm the driver, vehicle, pickup order and route plan the previous evening. In the morning, confirm that the ride is still operating and that each participant still intends to travel. Silence should not be treated as confirmation.
  • Do not make unannounced substitutions. If the driver, vehicle or passenger list changes, tell the whole group clearly and give every rider a straightforward option to decline. Fog makes visual recognition harder; confusion about who is expected in the vehicle should not become normal.
  • Set a delay rule. If the driver cannot safely reach the pickup point within the agreed window, delay or cancel the ride rather than repeatedly diverting through side roads to recover time. Name one person and one group channel for updates.
  • Make opting out consequence-free. A driver who cannot see adequately, or a passenger who does not feel safe reaching the pickup point, should be able to cancel without having to justify the choice to a manager or colleagues.

A Gurugram-to-Delhi example

Imagine four colleagues who usually travel from Gurugram to a Delhi office. On a normal day, two are collected from informal kerbside points and another from a market road. During a dense-fog warning, a safer plan may be to use one familiar residential-gate pickup point, confirm the vehicle before departure, and agree that a rider who misses the meeting window will not be chased through side streets.

That arrangement may mean a later arrival. It may also mean that the group does not travel at all. Neither outcome is a failure of commute planning. The failure is treating a routine arrival time as more important than a driver’s ability to see and make safe decisions.

What employers should own

Employers cannot control the weather or guarantee a safe road. They can control workplace expectations. For teams that use voluntary shared rides, the useful employer actions are modest: communicate attendance flexibility early, avoid penalising weather-related delay, make sure team leads know who can authorise a late start or remote work, and avoid requiring employees to disclose more location information than is necessary to coordinate a ride.

This matters particularly for employees travelling before daylight, across highway corridors or after shifts with little flexibility. A commute plan that works only when visibility is good is not a reliable plan for the winter season.

Where a carpool app can help—and where it cannot

A coordination tool can make a voluntary shared commute easier to verify and update, but it is not a substitute for weather-aware judgment. If a group uses TrustRoute, its public download page describes verified profiles, fixed fares and an option to choose women-only rides. Those features may help riders establish who is expected to travel and make a clearer plan before leaving home. ([trustroute.app](https://trustroute.app/download/))

They do not guarantee safety, make a poor pickup point safe, or justify continuing a trip when visibility is inadequate. Every rider and driver should still be able to delay or cancel. The appropriate role of a platform is to reduce avoidable uncertainty around a voluntary ride—not to override personal judgment or an employer’s responsibility to allow flexibility.

A fog-day checklist for commuters

  • Check the current IMD warning before committing to a longer trip; IMD’s fog portal also provides forecast layers for highway, rail and aviation segments. ([dss.imd.gov.in](https://dss.imd.gov.in/dwr_img/GIS/fogwarningportal/main.html))
  • Tell the group early if you are delaying, opting out or unable to drive.
  • Use one confirmed pickup point and one group communication channel.
  • Verify any changed driver, vehicle or passenger before travel; do not rely on a vague forwarded message.
  • Keep pickup waits away from moving traffic, medians and poorly visible road edges.
  • Do not ask a driver to make up lost time. In low visibility, a safe trip may take longer—or may need to wait.

The takeaway: Dense fog tests a commute before the vehicle is even moving. Flexible attendance, a fixed and safe pickup point, clear confirmation and a guilt-free option not to travel can remove much of the avoidable risk. Technology can support better coordination, but the most important safety feature is permission to slow down, delay or stay put when conditions demand it.
