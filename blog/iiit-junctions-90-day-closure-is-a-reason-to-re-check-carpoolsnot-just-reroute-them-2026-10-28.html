IIIT Junction’s 90-day closure is a reason to re-check carpools—not just reroute them

The renewed closure between DLF Gate No. 1 and Gachibowli Traffic Police Station changes more than a driving route. For Hyderabad offices, it can alter viable pickup points, return-trip timing and which voluntary carpools still make practical sense. A short, privacy-conscious re-match can reduce confusion without making shared travel compulsory.

A diversion notice tells drivers where to go. It does not tell colleagues whether their existing carpool can still work.

Cyberabad Traffic Police has extended the closure of the stretch between DLF Gate No. 1 and Gachibowli Traffic Police Station for a further 90 days from October 27, 2026, for construction of the multi-level flyover and underpass at IIIT Junction. The revised movements affect common approaches to DLF Road, IIIT Junction and Gachibowli. For offices in this corridor, the immediate task is not simply to circulate a map: it is to check whether voluntary shared-commute arrangements still have safe, realistic pickup and return plans. Reported traffic advisory

Why a route closure can break a carpool

A carpool depends on more than two people heading in roughly the same direction. It depends on a driver being able to collect a rider without a long detour, a meeting point where neither person needs to stop or wait unsafely, and a return journey that works after the office day ends.

The current arrangements are directional. Traffic from Radisson Hotel towards IIIT Junction is permitted only up to DLF Gate No. 1 and is directed onward via Gachibowli Junction and Indiranagar. Traffic coming from the Lingampally side towards DLF Road is diverted at Gachibowli Traffic Police Station via IIIT Junction, Gachibowli Junction and Radisson Junction. TCS employees using the Non-SEZ (CMC) gate may continue to use their regular gate. Diversion details

That means an old match may now fail on only one leg of the trip. A morning pickup may remain reasonable while an evening collection becomes unreliable; a rider who was previously on the way may now require a loop through a congested approach. At the same time, employees who did not formerly share a route may now have a practical common approach through Gachibowli or Indiranagar.

That is why this is a matching problem as well as a traffic-management problem.

Ask for a limited voluntary reset

Employers do not need employees’ precise home addresses to respond well. HR, facilities or transport teams can invite a voluntary update for the duration of the closure, collecting only information needed to identify workable groups:

  • broad origin area or corridor;
  • office gate or campus destination;
  • usual arrival and departure windows;
  • whether the employee is open to driving, riding or neither; and
  • any need for a different late-shift or hybrid-day arrangement.

The important safeguard is data minimisation. Do not publish home pins, personal phone numbers or commuting patterns in open spreadsheets or large chat groups. Use broad corridor-based matching first, and let employees decide whether to share more information with a potential match.

Match by the journey that is possible now

Old locality-based groups can be misleading during a long diversion. A better test is whether people can reach the same office gate within a reasonable arrival window without turning one person’s pickup into an extra cross-city trip.

For this closure, an employer could organise voluntary interest groups around:

  • Radisson–Gachibowli–Indiranagar–IIIT: for commuters using the notified approach towards IIIT Junction;
  • Lingampally–IIIT–Gachibowli–Radisson–DLF: for commuters whose DLF access follows the advised diversion;
  • common office-gate arrivals: where employees can share the final approach even if their earlier route differs; and
  • shift-specific windows: separating morning, evening and late-shift journeys instead of assuming every employee keeps the same schedule.

These are planning clusters, not prescribed routes. Drivers remain responsible for following live police directions and making their own safe, lawful driving decisions.

Check pickup points on the ground

A pin marked “near DLF” is not an operating plan. Construction and diversions can make a familiar point inaccessible, difficult to find or unsafe for a brief stop. A useful employer checklist is straightforward:

  1. Is stopping permitted and practical without blocking a moving lane?
  2. Can a rider reach the point without an unsafe crossing?
  3. Is the landmark clear in daylight and after dark?
  4. Is there a backup point if congestion makes the first point unusable?
  5. Does the driver have a safe way to continue if the rider is not ready?

Where an employer suggests a meeting area, it should describe the accessible side of the diversion, a recognisable landmark and a backup instruction. It should not imply that the company controls a public road or can guarantee conditions there.

Design the return trip before the first shared ride

Return journeys are often less predictable than morning arrivals. Meetings overrun, rain changes traffic conditions and employees may exit through a different gate. Before starting a new carpool, participants should agree on four basics:

  • the normal return departure window;
  • how long the group can wait before using a fallback;
  • the exact return pickup point; and
  • what each person will do if the driver cancels or is delayed.

A fallback need not be elaborate. It could be another willing colleague on the corridor, an employer shuttle where one exists, public transport, or an independently arranged ride. The purpose is to avoid treating a missed pickup as someone else’s problem.

Keep participation voluntary and privacy-conscious

Carpooling is not a substitute for public transport, traffic enforcement or a fair attendance policy during disruption. It should remain voluntary. Employees who prefer to travel alone, use transit, work different hours or opt out for personal safety reasons should not lose parking access, attendance flexibility or other support as a result.

A practical 10-day response

  • Days 1–2: Share the official diversion information, identify affected gates and invite a voluntary commute update.
  • Days 3–4: Create broad corridor and shift clusters; remove old pickup suggestions that now create avoidable detours.
  • Days 5–7: Check proposed meeting points during actual arrival and departure periods, then revise instructions based on observed conditions.
  • Week 2: Help willing drivers and riders re-match; publish fallback options and one clear contact for workplace transport issues.
  • Weekly: Revisit the plan as construction conditions, office attendance and police directions change.

The takeaway: A 90-day closure is long enough for old commute habits to become costly and confusing. Hyderabad employers cannot remove the bottleneck at IIIT Junction, but they can reduce avoidable friction by treating shared commuting as a voluntary coordination exercise: match on the routes that remain workable, use lawful meeting points, plan the return trip and protect employee privacy.
