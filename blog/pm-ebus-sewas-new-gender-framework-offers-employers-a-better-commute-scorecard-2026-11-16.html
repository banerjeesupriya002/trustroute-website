PM-eBus Sewa’s new gender framework offers employers a better commute scorecard

MoHUA’s October 2026 gender-action framework is for city bus systems, not employers. But its discipline—baseline the journey, measure specific failures and review outcomes—gives Indian workplaces a better way to improve access without treating a nearby station or a carpool app as a complete solution.

A Metro station near an office is an asset. It is not, by itself, proof that the commute works.

For many employees, especially those starting early or leaving after dark, the deciding part of a trip is the connection: the walk to a stop, a wait in an exposed place, a missed transfer, or the final stretch from a station to an office gate. An employer cannot run the city’s bus network or repair every footpath. It can, however, stop treating those failures as invisible.

That is the useful lesson in the Ministry of Housing and Urban Affairs’ October 2026 Guiding Framework for Gender Action Plan under PM-eBus Sewa. The framework is directed at cities and bus agencies—not companies—but it offers a practical measurement discipline that corporate mobility teams can adapt: establish a baseline, identify the specific weak link, set a realistic response and check whether it improved the journey. ([pm-ebus-sewa.mohua.gov.in](https://pm-ebus-sewa.mohua.gov.in/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/Gender-Action-Plan_Published_30.10.2026.pdf))

What the framework actually says

The document has three operational pillars: accessibility and affordability; safety and security; and workforce participation. Across them are 16 indicators—four, six and six respectively. It does not impose a single national target. Instead, it asks cities to select relevant indicators, establish baseline information, set annual targets and revisit them through gender audits. That matters: a checklist without a baseline can become a list of good intentions. ([pm-ebus-sewa.mohua.gov.in](https://pm-ebus-sewa.mohua.gov.in/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/Gender-Action-Plan_Published_30.10.2026.pdf))

Several indicators concern the physical and informational conditions around a bus trip: shelters, tactile flooring, ramps, seating and displayed route information at stops; lighting, CCTV, helpline visibility and clear sightlines at stops; and a grievance mechanism measured partly by the share of complaints resolved within 15 days. These are public-transport standards, not a template for office surveillance. But they make a useful distinction between a claimed amenity and a condition that can be observed and reviewed. ([pm-ebus-sewa.mohua.gov.in](https://pm-ebus-sewa.mohua.gov.in/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/Gender-Action-Plan_Published_30.10.2026.pdf))

Why this matters to Indian workplaces

India’s first National Household Travel Survey shows why a generic “near transit” claim is too weak. The National Statistical Office found that two-wheelers were the primary mode for 42.6% of workers travelling to fixed workplaces, while the average monthly commuting spend was ₹785 nationally and ₹1,044 in urban areas. The survey only records trips longer than one kilometre, so it should not be read as a complete picture of every doorstep journey. It is, however, a strong reason to examine the connections around the main mode rather than assume a single mode solves the trip. ([pib.gov.in](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleseDetailm.aspx?PRID=2319180&lang=1®=3&utm_source=openai))

The gender case is equally practical. The PM-eBus framework draws on the same survey to show that, in urban areas, 21.7% of women workers used buses as their primary commute mode, compared with 6.9% of men. That does not mean all women commute alike, or that a workplace should make decisions on anyone’s behalf. It does mean that poor waiting conditions, unreliable transfers and inaccessible stops can have unequal consequences for access to work. ([pm-ebus-sewa.mohua.gov.in](https://pm-ebus-sewa.mohua.gov.in/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/Gender-Action-Plan_Published_30.10.2026-1-1.pdf?utm_source=openai))

Use a commute scorecard, not individual tracking

An employer can borrow the framework’s logic without collecting employees’ precise home addresses or building a database of individual movements. Start with a voluntary, privacy-conscious survey and group results by broad corridor, shift window and usual mode. Then test the recurring problems that employees identify.

A useful site-level scorecard asks:

  • Approach: Is the walk between a major stop or station and the workplace continuous, usable and reasonably lit at the relevant shift time?
  • Wait: Is there a sheltered, visible place to wait? Are route and pickup instructions understandable?
  • Transfer: Which connections routinely fail at early, late or rotating shifts—and what happens when one is missed?
  • Office edge: Is there a clearly marked, pedestrian-safe pickup and drop-off point that does not force people to wait on a carriageway?
  • Response: Can an employee report a recurring commute problem, and does someone own the follow-up?
  • Outcome: After a change, did the reported wait, missed-connection risk or unsafe walking exposure actually fall?

The important unit of analysis is the recurring problem, not the named employee. A heat map of common shift-and-corridor gaps can justify changes such as a better-lit gate approach, a protected pickup bay, a revised shift start, a shuttle connection or a published late-service fallback. Managers do not need access to individual trip histories to make those decisions.

Keep responsibilities clear

A workplace should record civic defects—such as a broken footpath, missing crossing or dark bus stop—and raise them with the appropriate local authority. It should not imply that an internal commute survey transfers responsibility for public infrastructure to employees or makes the employer the guarantor of off-campus safety.

Its own responsibilities are more immediate: fair attendance treatment when a known connection fails, workable shift timing, safe campus access, a clear escalation route and suitable transport arrangements where the nature of work requires them. The PM-eBus framework’s grievance indicator is a useful reminder that a helpline or reporting channel is only meaningful when reports have an owner and a visible resolution standard. ([pm-ebus-sewa.mohua.gov.in](https://pm-ebus-sewa.mohua.gov.in/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/Gender-Action-Plan_Published_30.10.2026.pdf))

Where voluntary carpooling belongs

Carpooling can help with a narrow set of persistent gaps: for example, a 6:30 am shift before regular services begin, or a workplace whose final connection is unreliable. It should not become a substitute for public transport investment, a late-shift transport policy, or an employee’s right to decline a shared ride.

Where colleagues already make similar trips, a structured, opt-in carpool can be more workable than an informal chat group. TrustRoute says it uses Aadhaar eKYC and official-email verification for members; car owners also complete driving-licence and vehicle-registration checks. Its app presents fixed fares, direct rider-to-owner UPI payment, trip-status and reliability signals, plus women-only matching where users prefer it. Those features can improve clarity before a ride is accepted, but they are aids to judgement—not a safety guarantee—and TrustRoute does not operate vehicles or provide taxi services. ([trustroute.app](https://trustroute.app/))

The better corporate mobility measure is therefore not “how close is the office to transit?” It is “which repeatable parts of the journey stop people from getting to work reliably, and what can this workplace responsibly change?” MoHUA’s new framework provides the measurement habit. Employers should use it to fix the walk, wait, transfer and office edge first; voluntary carpooling can then remain one carefully bounded option for gaps that still persist.
