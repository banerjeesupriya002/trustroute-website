Bengaluru’s paused flyover curbs show why safety needs a workable alternative

Bengaluru has paused planned two-wheeler and autorickshaw restrictions on the Airport Road and Electronics City elevated corridors after diversion-related congestion emerged. The lesson is not to abandon road safety, but to test whether the whole replacement journey is safe and workable before a restriction takes effect.

Road safety cannot be judged only by what happens on a flyover. It must be judged by what happens to the commuter after they are diverted.

That is the practical lesson from Bengaluru’s recent restrictions on five major flyovers and elevated corridors. Bengaluru Traffic Police announced the measures after considering accident cases, congestion, flyover design and breakdown-related disruption. Restrictions began on September 4, 2026, on several corridors, while separate restrictions for the Electronics City elevated corridor had initially been scheduled for September 11. The Indian Express reported the original notification and corridor-wise plan.

The Airport Road experience quickly showed why implementation details matter. Authorities subsequently put the planned two-wheeler and autorickshaw curbs on the Airport Road and Electronics City elevated corridors on hold, adopting a phased approach instead. According to the Karnataka home minister’s statement reported on September 9, diverting goods vehicles, two-wheelers and autorickshaws at Airport Road entry points had created queues, congestion and traffic conflicts on the surface route. Goods-vehicle restrictions on that Airport Road stretch were to continue, while two-wheelers and autos remained permitted on the elevated corridor for the time being. The New Indian Express

This is not evidence that two-wheeler safety is unimportant. The original policy was explicitly prompted by safety and traffic-management concerns, and reporting on the pilot cited fatal crashes involving two-wheelers on both the Airport Road and Electronics City corridors. The South First reported that the Airport Road elevated corridor recorded five fatal crashes in 2025 and 10 in the first seven months of 2026; the Electronics City corridor recorded 14 fatal two-wheeler crashes in 2025 and 12 in the same period of 2026.

The stronger conclusion is that a restriction needs to lower risk across the entire trip, not simply remove a vehicle category from one elevated section.

A diversion is not automatically an alternative

For commuters, “use the service road” is not a complete mobility plan. A credible alternative must be navigable before the decision point, have enough capacity at peak times, offer safe merges and crossings, and provide a realistic way to reach offices, homes, bus stops or Metro stations. It must also work in rain and for workers travelling outside conventional office hours.

This matters especially in Bengaluru, where two-wheelers often fill gaps left by indirect transit, difficult last-mile connections and rigid reporting times. A rider displaced from an elevated corridor may face more junctions, more stop-start traffic and less predictable travel time below it. Those outcomes do not automatically make a safety restriction wrong, but they do mean authorities should measure whether risk or delay has merely shifted to another part of the network.

Early enforcement also carried an operational cost. Reports from the first day described banners, announcements and police personnel diverting vehicles at affected flyovers; officers said continuous enforcement was difficult because entry points needed round-the-clock attention. The New Indian Express reported on the September 4 rollout. That is an important design constraint: if a rule depends on intensive manual control at every ramp, it should be trialled with staffing, signage and diversion capacity that match the task.

What a better safety pilot would measure

A phased pilot is more useful when the public can see what is being tested. Before broad two-wheeler restrictions return, authorities could publish corridor-specific baselines and review dates covering three questions:

  • Safety: Are fatal and serious crashes, speeding complaints, dangerous stopping and breakdown-clearance times improving?
  • Displacement: What happens to queue length, travel time and conflict points on service roads, ramps and adjoining junctions?
  • Access: Can nearby bus stops, Metro connections, footpaths, crossings and office approaches safely accommodate the changed travel pattern?

The answer may differ by corridor. A restriction suitable for a narrow or high-conflict structure may not translate directly to another flyover. Alongside access curbs, authorities can assess targeted measures such as clearer advance signs, speed management, enforcement against hazardous stopping, rapid breakdown removal and engineering improvements at risky entry, exit and merge points.

Transparent review does not dilute enforcement. It gives commuters a clearer reason to comply and makes it easier to distinguish a temporary operating problem from a measure that is demonstrably reducing harm.

Employers should plan before employees reach the diversion

For offices near affected corridors, a traffic restriction is also a workplace-planning issue. A generic message asking employees to “leave early” is rarely enough. Employers can identify affected neighbourhoods and shifts, share verified official route updates, stagger arrival windows where feasible and avoid locating pickup or drop-off points directly beside known choke points.

They should also treat public transport and shared rides as options rather than mandates. Metro or bus travel may be practical for some employees but not for everyone, particularly where the first or final leg is unreliable or where shift timing is difficult. Similarly, a voluntary carpool can help colleagues travelling from similar areas reduce the number of separate vehicle trips during a sudden route change, but it is not a replacement for safe streets or adequate public transport.

TrustRoute can fit narrowly into that voluntary layer: where available, it may help colleagues coordinate a regular shared commute. Users and employers should assess the service’s currently available features and make their own decisions about route, driver, vehicle and trip suitability. No carpooling platform should be presented as a guarantee of personal safety or as a substitute for road design, enforcement and emergency response.

The real test is total harm, not initial popularity

Road-safety measures will sometimes be inconvenient. The relevant question is not whether every restriction is popular; it is whether it reduces overall harm while leaving people a safe, understandable and workable way to complete the journey.

Bengaluru’s decision to pause parts of the plan is therefore useful feedback. The city has identified serious concerns on elevated corridors, but the Airport Road rollout demonstrated that surface-road readiness cannot be an afterthought. Before the next phase, the practical priority is to test the diversion, communicate it clearly and measure what changes for the people who must use it every day.

Takeaway: moving two-wheelers off one road is not, by itself, a complete safety intervention. Safer commuting requires managing the full trip—from the approach road and diversion point to the last mile at work or home.
