Smart commuting needs useful data boundaries—not routine surveillance

Bhubaneswar’s Urban Mobility India conference highlighted real-time data, safe mobility and first/last-mile connections. The commuter-level test is more exacting: technology should make a trip easier to coordinate while clearly limiting who can see personal location and trip data, for what purpose and for how long.

At 8:35 on a weekday morning, intelligent mobility is not a control room full of dashboards. It is knowing whether a pickup is on time, where to meet, what the agreed cost is and whether a delayed shared ride will break a Metro connection.

That is the standard commuters should use when assessing any carpool, shuttle or ride-matching tool: does it solve a real coordination problem without quietly turning a daily routine into a permanent record?

The question is timely after the 19th Urban Mobility India Conference and Exhibition, held in Bhubaneswar on October 23–24, 2026. The Ministry of Housing and Urban Affairs said the event drew around 1,500 delegates. Its discussions covered first- and last-mile connections, walkable streets, data-informed planning and a round table on intelligence-driven safe and secure mobility.

Useful intelligence is felt at the kerb

At the conference, the Union Minister described integration, inclusiveness and intelligence as future pillars of urban mobility. The government’s account links intelligence to AI and real-time data for traffic management, passenger safety and public-transport efficiency. Those are worthwhile system goals. But their public value depends on what happens in the small moments of a journey.

For someone sharing a car to a station or office, useful information is simple: the co-traveller’s relevant identity and vehicle details, a confirmed pickup point, an agreed cost, a visible trip status and a clear way to report or cancel a problem. A live location can be useful for the limited period in which two people are trying to find each other or manage a delay.

That does not automatically justify collecting a continuous history of a person’s route, workplace attendance pattern or non-work travel. The difference matters especially for women, shift workers and employees whose commute can reveal where they live and when they travel alone.

Five questions before sharing commute data

Whether the service is supplied by an employer, a transport operator or a carpool platform, commuters should be able to get clear answers to five questions:

  1. What problem does this data solve? “Safety” and “convenience” are too broad on their own. A platform should distinguish route matching, pickup coordination, live trip sharing, support and fraud prevention.
  2. Who can see my location? The answer should separate fellow riders, the platform, an employer and outside service providers. These audiences should not be treated as interchangeable.
  3. When does location sharing start and stop? A feature used during an active trip is materially different from background tracking throughout the day.
  4. What can another rider see before booking? Enough information should be available to make an informed decision, but that does not require exposing a phone number, precise home address or unnecessary personal details to everyone browsing a route.
  5. How long is trip information retained, and can I access, correct or delete my account data? A clear retention and deletion policy is as important as a clear pickup policy.

India’s Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 is built around a balance: protecting an individual’s personal data while allowing processing for lawful purposes. The law and its staged implementation do not remove the need for good product judgement today. A practical commute service should still collect only what it can explain, restrict access to the people who need it and make its boundaries understandable before a person joins a ride.

What employers should—and should not—measure

Employers can learn a great deal from commute data without monitoring every employee. Aggregate, privacy-protective patterns may reveal that a shuttle stop is poorly placed, a shift change overloads one gate or a Metro connection routinely fails after a certain hour. That can support better pickup points, staggered arrivals or a safer walking connection.

But a voluntary commute programme should not become a condition for handing over minute-by-minute movement data. Employers should define the purpose of any data collection, restrict access, set a retention period and make clear that an employee’s manager cannot use commute information as a proxy for attendance or performance. A mobility benefit works best when participation is genuinely voluntary and people who need to travel alone are not disadvantaged.

Verification helps, but it is not a safety guarantee

Identity and vehicle checks can help a commuter make a more informed decision than an anonymous group chat. They cannot guarantee that every trip will be safe, punctual or comfortable. The same is true of ratings, route histories and women-only matching: each can be a useful layer, but none replaces personal judgement, emergency support, safer streets or reliable public transport.

For Indian cities, the larger priority remains a complete journey: dependable buses and rail, safe walking and waiting areas, workable first- and last-mile links, and clear information when disruption changes the plan. A voluntary carpool can supplement those options when people already have overlapping routes; it should not be presented as a substitute for them.
