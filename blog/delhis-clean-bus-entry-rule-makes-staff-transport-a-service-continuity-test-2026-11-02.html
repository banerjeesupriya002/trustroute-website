Delhi’s clean-bus entry rule makes staff transport a service-continuity test

From November 1, 2026, buses entering Delhi generally must be CNG, electric or BS-VI diesel under CAQM Direction No. 93, unless registered in Delhi. For NCR employers using cross-border staff buses, the immediate task is vehicle-level verification and a fair fallback plan—not an attendance warning.

A staff bus that cannot lawfully enter Delhi can quickly become an employee-support problem. For offices drawing people from Gurugram, Noida, Ghaziabad, Faridabad and Sonipat, a disruption to a contracted route can mean missed connections, delayed shifts and unequal access to the workplace. That makes cleaner-fleet compliance more than a procurement checkbox: it is part of commute continuity.

CAQM Direction No. 93 took effect on November 1, 2026. It says that buses entering Delhi must be CNG, electric or BS-VI diesel, including buses operating under All India Tourist Permit and other service regimes. Buses registered in Delhi are excluded from this particular restriction. The Direction asks Delhi’s Transport Department and Traffic Police to ensure compliance at border points through appropriate mechanisms such as ANPR and RFID. Read CAQM Direction No. 93 ([caqm.nic.in](https://caqm.nic.in/WriteReadData/LINKS/Direction%20No-93b0619ba1-8d40-4e70-a69b-401c29811ec3.pdf))

The rule plainly matters to contract-carriage staff buses. CAQM specifically identified contract-carriage, institutional and school-bus permit regimes among the buses entering Delhi that needed to shift to cleaner modes. Haryana’s Faridabad transport administration has separately asked operators to check each Delhi-bound bus’s registration certificate, fuel type and emission standard, and not run sub-BS-VI diesel buses on Delhi routes after October 31, 2026. Read the Haryana advisory ([prms.prharyana.gov.in](https://prms.prharyana.gov.in/press-release/8854))

The important distinction: a transport rule is not an attendance policy

Direction No. 93 regulates entry into Delhi; it does not prescribe how an employer should record attendance, reimburse a disrupted trip or redeploy a worker whose notified shuttle fails. Those are workplace decisions.

That distinction is important. If an employee reaches the assigned pickup point but the company’s contracted service cannot proceed, treating the resulting delay as individual misconduct is hard to justify. The operational failure may sit with the supplier, but the employee consequence sits with the employer. A fair response should be decided before the next morning peak, rather than improvised by individual managers.

There is also a practical equity issue. A generic instruction to “take Metro” may work for some corridors, but not necessarily for a 6 am start, a late return, an industrial-area site or a commuter facing a long and poorly connected final mile. The best fallback is therefore route-specific—not a blanket message that shifts all cost and uncertainty to employees.

What employers should verify now

1. Check every vehicle, not just the vendor.
Build a register for every bus scheduled to cross into Delhi: registration number, registration jurisdiction, fuel type, emission standard, permit category and assigned route. Ask for the registration certificate and supporting records. A supplier-wide assurance is not evidence that the specific replacement bus arriving tomorrow is eligible.

2. Treat the Delhi-registration carve-out narrowly.
Direction No. 93 excludes buses registered in Delhi from this restriction. It does not mean that a contractor with a partly Delhi-registered fleet can treat all of its routes as cleared, nor does it displace other applicable permit, fitness, insurance or road-safety obligations. Audit substitute vehicles as carefully as the regular roster.

3. Separate the legal eligibility check from routine fleet checks.
For this entry rule, the central questions are the vehicle’s registration, fuel and emission standard. Permit validity, fitness, insurance and PUC status remain sensible operational checks, but employers should not confuse them with proof that a non-Delhi bus meets Direction No. 93.

4. Map the route’s actual border dependency.
List which services enter Delhi, where they enter, their first-pickup times and the realistic alternatives if the regular vehicle cannot operate. A Noida-to-Gurugram route that normally crosses Delhi has a different contingency problem from a Gurugram-to-central-Delhi route. Test whether the suggested interchange is genuinely reachable and safe at the relevant shift time.

5. Require a written service-recovery plan.
Ask the transport provider for compliant standby capacity, named escalation contacts, vehicle-change notification rules and a route-by-route recovery timeline. “We will arrange another bus” is not a usable plan when hundreds of people are waiting across multiple pickup points.

Set the employee fallback before a service fails

A short written protocol can prevent arbitrary decisions. It should state who may work remotely, how much reporting-time flexibility applies, when an employee can use an approved alternative, how a manager records the exception, and who authorises exceptional last-mile reimbursement. The principle should be simple: comparable commute constraints should receive comparable treatment.

Communications matter as much as the policy. Employees need one early, plain-language update: whether their service is operating; what has changed; whether they should travel, use a named interchange, work remotely or report later; and whom to contact if the proposed alternative is not workable. Transport, facilities, security and HR should use the same message rather than send conflicting instructions. Where drivers or a substantial part of the workforce use Hindi or another local language, the operational notice should be available in that language too.

Employers should take extra care with late-shift workers and women commuters. Public transport may be the best mass alternative on many routes, but it is not a complete answer if the viable service ends before a shift does, or if the remaining walk and wait are unsuitable. A fallback plan should identify those gaps rather than assume every affected employee can absorb them alone.

Where voluntary carpooling fits—and where it does not

Voluntary carpooling can help a limited set of commuters who make recurring trips at similar times and have a practical shared corridor. It is not a substitute for a compliant staff-bus operation, and it should never be made a condition of attendance.

Employers considering it should keep participation opt-in, avoid publishing personal contact details unnecessarily, explain any reimbursement treatment and make clear that employees may decline. For people who independently choose a recurring shared ride, TrustRoute describes features including verified profiles, fixed fares, route matching and women-only ride options. Those tools may help commuters coordinate a voluntary ride more clearly; they do not guarantee safety, replace public transport or relieve an employer of managing its own contracted fleet.

What not to do

  • Do not accept a vendor email as the only proof that every bus is eligible to enter Delhi.
  • Do not apply late marks or leave deductions before checking whether the notified company transport failed.
  • Do not require employees to use private cars or ride with colleagues to preserve attendance.
  • Do not present a distant Metro station as a complete contingency without checking the first and last mile by shift.
  • Do not assume the Delhi-registration exception applies across a contractor’s fleet.

The takeaway: Direction No. 93 is an air-quality measure, but its near-term workplace consequence is a transport-reliability test. NCR employers should document eligibility vehicle by vehicle, secure credible standby arrangements and make fair attendance decisions when a company-arranged route breaks down. That protects employees while supporting the intended transition to cleaner buses.
