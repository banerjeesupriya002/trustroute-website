When roadworks scramble the last mile, an office fallback plan beats a “leave early” email

A short resurfacing job can disrupt the part of the commute that employees have least room to improvise: the link between a station, shuttle stop or drop-off point and the office. A practical contingency plan names safe alternate pickup points, communicates early and preserves voluntary options when the normal route fails.

Roadworks do not have to shut an expressway to derail an office commute. A resurfacing patch, narrowed access road or queue outside a Metro exit can disrupt the most fragile part of the trip: the final connection between public transport, a shuttle stop or a drop-off point and the workplace.

Delhi offers a current example. On September 23, 2026, Delhi Traffic Police advised commuters to expect morning-peak delays for two days on the DND-to-Ashram stretch while PWD resurfacing was under way. Its traffic-alert feed also warned of up to 15 days of slow movement on SSN Marg and Dera Mandi Road because of re-carpeting and maintenance work. The official advice—plan ahead, use alternate routes and follow traffic staff directions—is appropriate. For employers, however, the operational question is more specific: what happens after an employee’s usual feeder link, pickup point or walking route stops working?

A delayed final leg is not merely an inconvenience. An employee may exit at an unfamiliar gate, miss a shuttle, be dropped on the wrong side of a busy road or have to find an alternative after dark. Recent reporting from Delhi records women students describing unreliable evening buses, poorly lit stretches and harassment as factors in routine travel decisions. That does not mean every diverted trip is unsafe. It does mean that reliability and perceived safety are inseparable in last-mile planning.

Why “leave early” is not a fallback plan

A blanket instruction to leave early assumes that employees control their work finish time, childcare, train or bus connections, and choice of route. Many do not. It can simply shift the uncertainty: someone reaches a station earlier, then waits longer for a disrupted feeder service or has to make an unplanned walk.

Roadworks should instead be treated as a small operating event. The aim is not to promise a traffic-free commute or guarantee safety. It is to reduce avoidable uncertainty by preserving three things:

  • Clarity: employees know exactly where to go.
  • Choice: no one is forced into an unfamiliar route or ride to protect attendance.
  • Coordination: a changed pickup or delayed vehicle does not leave people relying on scattered messages.

1. Map alternate pickup points—not just alternate roads

For each office cluster, identify two or three contingency meeting points before disruption occurs. The best point is not automatically the closest one to the office. It should be easy to locate, lawful and practical for a brief vehicle stop, visibly staffed or well used, and reachable without requiring people to cross fast traffic or use an isolated shortcut.

That could mean an agreed Metro gate, an office lobby, or another location whose operator has permitted pickup activity. Facilities and security teams should test the route at the relevant shift times rather than choosing a point only from a map.

Give employees an exact map pin, approach direction and plain-language landmark. “Pickup near Ashram” is not an instruction. “Use the designated gate; wait beside the security desk rather than across the service road” is.

2. Communicate before the final leg begins

Timing is part of the control. Send an initial alert before the usual departure window, then issue a concise update only when the pickup point, route or departure time changes. One accountable channel is better than a mix of email, calls and informal WhatsApp messages.

Each update should answer five questions: what has changed, who is affected, which pickup point now applies, what happens if that point becomes inaccessible, and whom to contact. The message should also state the no-penalty alternative—such as delayed reporting, remote work where the role allows it, or manager-approved transport—when the fallback is not workable.

This matters because attendance policy can unintentionally create risk. If an employee believes an unfamiliar ride or poorly lit walking route is the only way to avoid being marked late, the company has shifted the problem rather than managed it.

3. Make colleague carpooling a voluntary contingency, not an improvised plea

When a feeder route, office shuttle or walking connection breaks, a voluntary colleague carpool can help bridge the gap for people travelling along similar corridors. But “Anyone going this way?” in a large chat group is not a reliable commute system.

A usable fallback makes the essentials visible: the broad route, departure time, seats available, pickup point and confirmation status. It must also preserve consent. No employee should be pressured to drive, accept a ride or change their normal route, and there should be a simple way to decline a proposed option privately.

TrustRoute can support this narrow coordination task. Its current App Store listing says it shows verified badges and reliability information, supports route and via-stop matching, and opens a temporary trip group chat shortly before departure. The listing also describes DigiLocker-based Aadhaar and driving-licence checks and direct UPI payment options. Those features can provide more context and structure than an ad-hoc group message; they do not guarantee a safe trip, replace employer transport obligations or make an unsuitable pickup point acceptable.

4. Keep trip coordination proportionate

A diversion often fails because a rider waits at the old point while the driver is redirected, phones run low and no one knows whether the trip has started. A trip-specific chat or agreed call protocol can solve much of this without turning a temporary disruption into workplace surveillance.

Employers should set clear boundaries: trip information should be available only to participating people, only for the active journey, and never repurposed for after-hours attendance tracking. The point is to coordinate a changed pickup—not to monitor private movement.

5. Design for women’s choice—and for everyone who has less room to improvise

The answer to a disrupted commute is not to tell women to be more careful. A better plan gives people options without requiring them to publicly explain why: a better-lit alternate point, a shared departure, a company-arranged shuttle where feasible, or a women-only carpool preference for those who want it.

The same design helps newer employees unfamiliar with the area, people with disabilities, staff carrying equipment and anyone whose public-transport connection has become unreliable. Specificity is what makes a contingency plan inclusive.

Turn roadwork alerts into a repeatable routine

Delhi Metro’s annual report describes integration of last-mile connectivity solutions with feeder services and door-to-door journey options. That is a useful reminder that a rail trip is only as dependable as its connections. Employers cannot repair city infrastructure during a two-day resurfacing project, but they can avoid creating a second layer of confusion at the point employees most need a clear plan.

  • Monitor: check official traffic and transit alerts for office corridors.
  • Assess: identify affected shifts, station exits, shuttle stops and feeder links—not only congested roads.
  • Activate: publish tested alternate pickup points and available voluntary travel options early.
  • Coordinate: use one route-specific communication channel for participants.
  • Escalate: provide a no-penalty alternative when the fallback itself is unsuitable.
  • Review: after the disruption, record which locations, messages and options actually worked.

The takeaway: roadworks are routine; stranded last-mile decisions should not be. Replace the generic “leave early” email with named and tested pickup points, timely updates, voluntary ride-sharing and a clear option not to travel when the fallback is not workable. TrustRoute may be useful as one coordination layer for voluntary colleague carpools, but the employer remains responsible for setting fair rules and avoiding pressure to take an unsafe or unsuitable option.
