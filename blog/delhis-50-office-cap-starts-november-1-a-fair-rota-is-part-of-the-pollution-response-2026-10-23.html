Corporate Mobility · 2026-10-23

Delhi’s 50% office cap starts November 1. A fair rota is part of the pollution response.

Delhi private offices must keep no more than 50% of staff physically at work from November 1, 2026, through January 31, 2027. The rule does not say who should commute, so employers need a transparent rota, real staggered hours and support for staff whose work cannot be done remotely.

Delhi’s winter workplace rule is not simply a work-from-home policy. It is a commute-allocation decision. From November 1, 2026, to January 31, 2027, private offices functioning in the NCT of Delhi must have no more than 50% of staff physically attending the workplace; the remaining staff must work from home. The same notification directs private entities to stagger working hours, comply with work-from-home norms and minimise office-commute vehicle movement through measures including public transport, common buses, ride-sharing and carpooling. Delhi Gazette notification

That leaves a decision the rule does not answer: who comes in, on which days, and under what conditions? If that choice is left to each manager, the likely result is not a cleaner or fairer commute. The same people can end up travelling every day because their work is less visible remotely, because they are junior, or because they find it harder to challenge a late roster change.

For employers, the immediate task is therefore not to build a last-minute attendance spreadsheet. It is to reduce avoidable trips while distributing the unavoidable ones by clear operational criteria.

Read the dates and scope correctly

The 50% physical-attendance ceiling for government and private offices runs from November 1, 2026, through January 31, 2027. Essential and emergency services are exempted, including health care, fire services, public transport, electricity, water, sanitation, disaster response and certain environmental-enforcement functions. An employer should not treat “essential” as a broad label for ordinary business preference; where the position is unclear, it should check the notification and obtain appropriate advice.

Other dates matter too. Authorised parking charges are to be doubled from November 1 through February 28, 2027, while parking owned and managed by Delhi Metro Rail Corporation is excluded. The government’s wider winter framework also runs through February 28. Akashvani’s report on the notified framework

The notification gives fixed timings for MCD and GNCTD offices, but for private employers the practical obligation is to implement staggered working hours. Merely moving the stated start time while continuing to schedule all meetings at 10 am does not meaningfully stagger travel.

Publish a rota, not a chain of exceptions

A workable approach is a two-week rolling rota with named cohorts, fixed office days and a stated basis for exceptions. That gives people enough notice to arrange childcare, connect with Metro or bus services, share a household vehicle, or decide whether a voluntary shared ride is practical.

Before publishing the rota, HR and operations teams should test four questions:

  • Task necessity: Does the work require physical access, a handover, equipment, a client interaction or on-site coverage—or is office attendance simply customary?
  • Frequency: Is one group being required to travel substantially more often than comparable colleagues?
  • Predictability: Can an employee see their office days early enough to plan the full journey?
  • Adjustment process: Is there a confidential route for employees to request a different day or arrival window because of disability, caregiving, a difficult multi-leg commute or a late-shift safety concern?

Equal treatment does not always mean identical office days. A facilities technician and a payroll analyst do different work. Fair treatment means that the difference is connected to a documented business need, reviewed regularly, and not determined by managerial preference or desk visibility.

Stagger the whole workplace, not just the clock

Private offices can use arrival bands, such as 8:30–9:00 am, 9:30–10:00 am and 10:30–11:00 am, but they must align the rest of the workplace with them. That means shifting recurring meetings, reception coverage, cafeteria demand, visitor appointments, security staffing and any shuttle timetable.

Facilities teams should also inspect the points where a commute turns into a queue: vehicle gates, basement ramps, drop-off areas, lift banks, footpaths and the path between the nearest transit stop and the office. A staggered-hours plan fails if everyone waits at the same gate or is forced into an unsafe walk after dark.

Parking is a useful test of whether the policy matches its purpose. Employers do not need to subsidise every solo car trip simply because public parking has become more expensive. Where they provide parking, they can state transparent priority for accessible travel, essential on-site roles, late shifts and genuinely shared vehicles—while keeping a workable option for employees who cannot safely or reasonably use another mode.

Do not make on-site staff carry the entire burden

The cap will make a longstanding workplace divide more visible. Some employees can work remotely; security, building services, IT support, reception, laboratory, operations and customer-facing staff may not be able to. A credible plan identifies those roles early and asks what would make their remaining trips more workable: a stable shift, a safer pickup point, a shuttle link, an ability to swap a rostered day with notice, or parking access for a shared trip.

For women and late-shift workers, “use public transport” is often incomplete advice. Public transport may be the best option for much of a journey, but an employer should also consider the first and last mile, waiting time after dark and the effect of a roster change sent the previous evening. Remote-work allocations should not become a mechanism by which the least flexible workers absorb the greatest commute exposure.

Use carpooling as an option, not as a duty-of-care substitute

The Delhi notification explicitly identifies carpooling and ride-sharing as ways to reduce office-related vehicle movement. But a carpool is only useful when it is voluntary, compatible with an employee’s route and shift, and arranged with enough notice. It should never be compulsory, nor should it be used to remove an employer’s responsibility for appropriate late-shift arrangements.

For employees who choose to share a regular commute, a structured tool can be safer and clearer than a public spreadsheet or an unverified messaging group. TrustRoute says it verifies member identity and official email, checks a car owner’s driving licence and vehicle registration certificate, shows fixed fares and reliability signals, and enables group chat and live-location coordination. It also offers women-only matching. These are decision-support features, not safety guarantees; employees should still review the trip and use their own judgement.

An employer’s role should be limited and privacy-conscious: publish rosters early, invite opt-in participation, avoid collecting precise home addresses in shared documents, and provide a contact for commute-related operational issues. Public transport, walking, cycling, shuttles and voluntary carpooling should be complementary choices, not a contest between modes.

A practical checklist before November 1

  1. Confirm whether the office is covered by the NCT notification and whether any essential-service exemption applies.
  2. Set an accountable senior owner for compliance, HR fairness and workplace operations.
  3. Classify roles by genuine on-site necessity rather than seniority or habit.
  4. Publish at least two weeks of 50%-compliant attendance cohorts before the rule begins.
  5. Create a confidential process for commute, care, accessibility and safety-related adjustment requests.
  6. Set genuine arrival bands and move meetings and site services to support them.
  7. Check gates, drop-off areas, parking, lighting and late-shift departure arrangements.
  8. Make any carpool or ride-sharing option voluntary, verified where possible and privacy-aware.
  9. Review the rota weekly: who is commuting, who is repeatedly asked to attend, and where the journey is breaking down.

The point is not to decide who deserves to work from home. It is to comply with a defined winter measure without concentrating the remaining commute burden on the people with the least room to refuse it. A published rota, credible staggering and practical support for on-site workers will do more than an attendance cap implemented by email.
