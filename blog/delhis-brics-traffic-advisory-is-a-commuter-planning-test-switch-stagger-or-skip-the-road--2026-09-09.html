Delhi’s BRICS traffic advisory is a commuter-planning test: switch, stagger or skip the road trip

Delhi Traffic Police has issued a traffic advisory for September 11–13 in connection with the BRICS Summit, while the leaders’ summit is scheduled for September 12–13 at Bharat Mandapam. For commuters, the useful response is not a blanket “leave early” rule: identify whether your route is exposed, use Metro where it removes the uncertain section, and ask employers to stagger flexible trips.

Delhi’s BRICS-related traffic arrangements are a reminder that a commute can fail at its most ordinary point: the final approach to the office, station, hospital or home. A road that is usually dependable may be regulated around dignitary movement; an alternate route may become busy precisely because many people choose it at once.

Delhi Traffic Police has published an advisory covering September 11–13, 2026 in connection with the 18th BRICS Summit. Separately, an Indian government press release says the leaders’ summit will be held at Bharat Mandapam, New Delhi, on September 12–13, 2026. The extra day in the traffic advisory matters: commuters with plans on Friday, September 11 should not assume disruption begins only when the formal summit opens. Delhi Traffic Police’s traffic-diversions page is the primary place to check the advisory and any updates.

The police advice is appropriately broad: plan journeys, follow directions on the road, consider Metro or public transport, and allow additional time. That is sound advice—but it is not a complete door-to-door plan for every Delhi-NCR commuter. Metro may be the clearest option for one trip, while a staggered arrival, remote-work arrangement or pre-coordinated shared car may be more realistic for another.

Start with the route, not the destination

Do not decide solely because your office is “in central Delhi” or because your destination is outside it. Look at the full journey: the approach roads, key junctions, airport or railway-station access, and the final kilometre after leaving Metro.

Reporting on the advisory identifies affected corridors across New Delhi and parts of central and south Delhi, including routes around Mathura Road, Janpath, Ashoka Road, Shanti Path, Sardar Patel Marg, Tilak Marg, Kartavya Path and A.P.J. Abdul Kalam Road. The specific restrictions and diversions can change with operational requirements, so a list from the previous evening should be treated as a planning prompt—not a guarantee that the route will remain open at a given time. The Indian Express’s report on the police plan provides useful context on the corridors and diversion points, but the live police advisory should take priority.

A practical way to assess exposure is to place your journey in one of three groups:

  • Directly exposed: your usual route, destination or final approach enters the regulated New Delhi corridor. Expect diversions and build a meaningful time buffer.
  • Indirectly exposed: you do not travel on a listed corridor but cross or feed into central Delhi. Your route may be affected by spillover traffic, particularly near major junctions.
  • Lower exposure: your commute remains outside the affected area. Check conditions before leaving, but do not take a long detour merely because there is a citywide advisory.

This distinction prevents a common mistake: treating every commuter as if they face the same problem. A person travelling from Dwarka to Connaught Place, a late-shift worker returning to Gurugram, and someone heading to IGI Airport have different constraints and different consequences if a trip runs late.

When Metro is the sensible switch

Metro is most useful when it replaces the road section that has become least predictable. If a station is practical at both ends and the route avoids an awkward late-night transfer, a rail-led trip can remove much of the uncertainty associated with crossing New Delhi by road.

Before switching, check three things:

  • Access: Can you reach the first station and leave the last station safely and predictably, without relying on a difficult last-minute cab search?
  • Timing: Does the route work for your shift, meeting or return journey, including interchange and walking time?
  • Last mile: Have you decided in advance how you will cover the distance from the station to the workplace or home?

For example, a Noida-to-Mandi House commuter with convenient station access may be better served by Metro than by trying to drive through central corridors. But Metro is not automatically the best answer for a commuter carrying equipment, travelling with a child, leaving after service frequencies reduce, or working at an office far from a station. “Use public transport” works only when the whole trip works.

When to stagger, rather than travel earlier

“Leave early” is sometimes necessary, especially for flights, trains, medical appointments and shift handovers. But for flexible office journeys, it can simply move more vehicles into the same already-stressed roads.

Employers can reduce that pressure by treating the advisory as a short business-continuity issue rather than an individual punctuality problem. For September 11–13, managers can consider:

  • arrival windows instead of one universal reporting time;
  • remote attendance for non-essential internal meetings;
  • temporary work-from-home or hybrid flexibility where the role permits it;
  • clear guidance for employees with fixed-time trips, late shifts or caregiving responsibilities; and
  • avoiding optional in-person events in the affected area during the advisory period.

The point is not to ask employees to absorb a volatile journey more efficiently. It is to remove trips that are flexible in the first place. That is particularly relevant in Delhi-NCR, where a cross-border commute from Gurugram, Noida, Ghaziabad or Faridabad often has fewer easy alternatives once a key corridor is disrupted.

When a shared car is the better road option

Some journeys will still need a car: a route with weak Metro access, a late shift, a trip with luggage or equipment, or a workplace beyond a practical station connection. On those days, the question is not whether traffic will disappear. It is whether people making very similar journeys can avoid creating several near-identical solo-car trips.

A carpool is most useful when the match is genuine: similar origin area, destination zone, departure window and tolerance for diversions. It is less useful when riders are scattered across a large catchment or have tightly conflicting schedules. Trying to add multiple detours to fill every seat can make the trip more fragile, not less.

For any shared ride—whether arranged among colleagues, through a residential community or on a carpool platform—agree on the pickup point, departure time, luggage needs, route expectations and cost before the morning rush. Use a service only if its verification and trip-information features meet your own comfort level, and keep a fallback option if a ride is cancelled or the route becomes impractical. Shared mobility should remain voluntary; it is not a substitute for safe public transport or an employer’s duty of care for late-shift staff.

TrustRoute can fit this limited use case by helping office commuters organise a voluntary carpool with people travelling in the same direction. It should not be presented as a way to bypass police restrictions, guarantee a ride or guarantee a faster journey. During traffic controls, its value is in clearer coordination when a road-based trip remains necessary—not in promising that congestion will vanish.

A 15-minute plan for Thursday night or Friday morning

  1. Check the official advisory. Review it the evening before and again before leaving. Do not rely on an old screenshot or a forwarded message.
  2. Identify the weak link. Is it the central road crossing, the station approach, the last mile, or access to the airport or railway station?
  3. Choose one primary mode. Use Metro if it removes the uncertain core of the trip; use a car only when it solves a real access or timing problem.
  4. Set a fallback. For a Metro trip, decide the final-leg option. For a shared-car trip, decide what happens if a rider or driver cancels.
  5. Separate fixed-time travel from flexible travel. Flights, trains, hospital visits and shift handovers need a larger buffer. A routine office arrival may be better handled through a changed reporting time.
  6. Follow instructions on the road. Navigation apps are useful, but traffic-police directions and temporary barriers reflect conditions that may not yet appear on a map.

The useful lesson goes beyond BRICS week

Major-event traffic controls expose a normal weakness in daily commuting: many journeys rely on one assumed road route and one assumed arrival time. The better question is not simply, “What is the fastest route?” It is, “Which part of this trip is now least reliable, and what reduces that risk?”

For some Delhi commuters on September 11–13, the answer will be Metro. For others, it will be a later start, remote work or a carefully planned shared car. The strongest plan is the one that recognises the limits of each option before the commute begins.
