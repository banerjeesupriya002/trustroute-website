A women’s commute safety audit should follow the whole trip—not stop at the office gate

The Supreme Court’s September 28 directions on public-space safety in Delhi-NCR reinforce a practical lesson for employers: a secure office campus cannot compensate for an unsafe walk, wait, transfer or last-mile connection. A useful commute audit follows the full shift journey and separates civic fixes from employer actions.

A well-lit lobby, guards at the gate and CCTV in a parking area all matter. But for a person leaving a late shift, those controls address only the final minutes of the journey.

The difficult questions begin earlier. Is the walk to the pickup point usable after dark? Does a bus stop remain visible and active when a shift ends? Can someone get from a Metro exit to the office without an isolated or poorly lit stretch? If a feeder service is missed or a shift overruns, is there a clear and voluntary fallback?

For employers with shift-based workforces, these are not peripheral questions. They affect whether employees can reliably take a role, accept a shift change or get home without being left to improvise when the normal trip breaks down.

A recent court order makes the public-infrastructure problem plain

In In Re: Recent Rape Incidents in Delhi NCR, decided on September 28, 2026 and uploaded by the Supreme Court of India on September 29, the Court directed Delhi-NCR authorities to undertake vulnerability mapping, improve patrolling at identified locations and carry out lighting and CCTV audits. The directions expressly include Metro stations, bus stops, bus terminals, railway stations and other major public-transport facilities in or adjoining identified vulnerable zones. The order also says that location-specific public complaints should be verified, acted on within a prescribed time frame and made trackable for the complainant.

The Supreme Court’s order is directed at public authorities, not private employers. The corporate implication is therefore an inference, not a legal restatement: an employer cannot repair a dark road or run a Metro station, but it should not pretend those gaps have no bearing on the safety and viability of its shift patterns.

This distinction matters. A workplace safety programme becomes performative if it audits the office perimeter while treating the walk, wait, transfer and final leg outside the gate as somebody else’s problem.

Why the whole journey matters in India

A commute is a chain, not a single mode. It may involve a walk to a stop, waiting, a bus or Metro ride, an interchange, another wait and a final walk or short vehicle trip. A weak link can make an otherwise good route unusable—particularly before early shifts, after late shifts, in heavy rain or when services become less frequent.

That is especially relevant to BPOs, hospitals, industrial sites, hotels, retail operations and logistics facilities. Their rosters may begin or end outside the busiest public-transport periods, when a station can be open but its surrounding street, feeder connection or pickup arrangement is far less dependable.

Indian evidence on gender-responsive transport supports looking beyond the vehicle itself. The World Bank recommends on-ground assessment of travel timing, routes, first- and last-mile access, crowding and safety concerns, and describes safety audits as a participatory way to identify problems in public spaces and transport facilities. Its Delhi example covered pavements, bus stops, Metro stations and last-mile connections. World Bank guidance on women-friendly public transport and the ITDP India–Safetipin policy brief both make the case for considering safety, comfort, convenience and affordability across the journey.

The key insight for employers is simple: proximity to a Metro station or bus corridor is not the same as a workable commute. The relevant test is whether the connection works at the actual shift time, in both directions, including when something goes wrong.

Run a route-and-shift audit, not a vague sentiment survey

Asking employees whether they “feel safe commuting” may identify concern, but it rarely tells a team what to fix. A stronger approach maps recurring legs of the journey through voluntary, privacy-conscious input, then tests the locations at the times people actually use them.

Start with the main origins and approaches used by groups of employees, rather than collecting precise home addresses. For each significant shift, examine four things:

  • Walking approaches: Where do people walk to reach the office, station, stop or pickup point? Check pavement continuity, crossings, obstructions, lighting, sightlines and whether the route remains usable during rain.
  • Waiting points: Where do people wait, and for how long? Note shelter, visibility, active surroundings, access to assistance and whether the point changes after the final scheduled bus or feeder service.
  • Transfers and last-mile links: Test station exits, bus-to-Metro changes and the final connection to the workplace. A station nearby is useful only if its connection is practical at 6 a.m., 10 p.m. or after an unexpected delay.
  • Exception cases: Ask what happens after a missed bus, a cancelled feeder, overtime, a roster change or heavy rain. The audit should assess the fallback, not only the ideal route.

Walk priority routes at the relevant time. A daytime inspection cannot establish whether an 11 p.m. pickup point is visible, staffed or connected to transport. Include women employees who volunteer to participate, along with facilities, security and transport coordinators. Do not require anyone to disclose a traumatic experience to establish that a location needs attention.

Use observable conditions rather than a simplistic “safe/unsafe” score: lighting continuity, sightlines, crossing risk, footpath condition, crowding, wayfinding, phone connectivity, service frequency and distance to a staffed location. The output should be a shift-wise list of priority locations and failure points—not an employee surveillance map. Keep data aggregated where possible, restrict access and retain it only for the stated purpose.

Turn findings into two accountable worklists

A credible audit separates public failures from actions the employer can take now.

First, create a civic escalation list. This can include broken streetlights, blocked footpaths, unsafe crossings, dark station approaches, non-functional cameras and badly placed bus stops. Record the precise location, date and relevant evidence, then submit the issue through the responsible civic, transit or police channel. This is where the Supreme Court’s emphasis on location-specific reports is useful: a vague complaint about an “unsafe area” is harder to investigate than a report tied to a particular crossing, stop or time window.

Second, create an employer action list. Possible responses include moving an office pickup point to a more active location, avoiding rosters that leave one person waiting alone for a long period, providing a staffed gate-to-vehicle escort where the risk assessment supports it, and publishing a clear contact route for late-shift transport failures. Managers should know in advance who can approve a safe alternative when a planned connection fails; employees should not have to argue their case while stranded.

These measures are not substitutes for functioning public transport, policing or street infrastructure. They are a practical response to the parts of the work journey an employer’s schedules and office location make foreseeable.

Where shared commuting can help—and where it cannot

An audit may show that public transport works for most of a journey but breaks down on one repeatable leg. For example, colleagues may have a reliable Metro trip but no dependable connection once late-evening feeders thin out. In another case, several employees may already travel along a common corridor but make separate, improvised arrangements from a station to home.

A voluntary shared-commute option can help with coordination on that specific weak link. It is not a safety guarantee, a replacement for safer streets and transit, or a reason to make women responsible for solving an infrastructure failure themselves.

Any such programme should keep participation voluntary; make timing and pickup arrangements clear; offer a visible reporting route; minimise unnecessary sharing of personal address information; and allow employees to decline without explanation or disadvantage. It should complement, rather than displace, transport support for people whose routes, care responsibilities, accessibility needs or shift times make carpooling unsuitable.

TrustRoute may be one voluntary coordination option for everyday office commuters. Its public product information describes verified profiles, route-based ride matching, fixed fares and women-only ride options. Those features can make it easier for people to assess and coordinate a shared commute, but they do not guarantee safety or remove the need for personal judgement, employer escalation processes and public-infrastructure fixes.

Review the system before an incident forces the issue

Commute conditions change with new shifts, office moves, transit diversions, roadworks and monsoon access problems. Review priority routes on a regular schedule and when employee feedback identifies a pattern. Track whether reported location issues were escalated, who owns the next step and how long it took to respond. Counting complaints alone can be misleading: fewer complaints may reflect resignation or under-reporting rather than safer travel.

The most useful test is practical: for every major shift, can an employee identify a realistic route, a safe enough waiting arrangement and a clear fallback if the expected connection fails?

The takeaway: gate security is necessary, but it cannot repair an unsafe or unreliable journey before the gate comes into view. Employers should map the whole commute, press public authorities to address public failures and offer voluntary support where a recurring first-, last-mile or late-shift gap is within their ability to reduce.
