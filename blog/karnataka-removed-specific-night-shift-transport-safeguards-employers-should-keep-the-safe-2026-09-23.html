Karnataka removed specific night-shift transport safeguards. Employers should keep the safety system.

Karnataka’s September 2026 amendment removed eight detailed night-shift safeguards for women employees from Section 25 of the Shops and Commercial Establishments Act. Core requirements—including written willingness, free transport with adequate security and GPS tracking—remain. For employers, that makes a clear transport operating system more important, not less.

GPS can show where a vehicle is. It cannot, on its own, show whether anyone will act when a journey goes wrong.

That is the practical issue for Karnataka employers after the Karnataka Shops and Commercial Establishments (Amendment) Act, 2026 took effect on September 4, 2026. The Act received the Governor’s assent on September 3 and omitted clauses 25(1)(h) to 25(1)(o) of the Karnataka Shops and Commercial Establishments Act, 1961. Those eight clauses had set out detailed transport and security requirements for women working night shifts. The published amendment text is clear on the clauses removed.

For employers with late-night operations in Bengaluru and elsewhere in Karnataka, the useful response is not to treat the shorter statutory checklist as a reason to shrink the transport programme. It is to decide which controls the organisation needs so that a colleague can get home with a plan that is understandable, auditable and responsive when plans change.

What the amendment removed

The omitted clauses had required employers to vet drivers; control changes to routes, drivers and shifts; protect women employees’ contact and address information; avoid routing that left one woman as both the first pickup and last drop; deploy guards in specified circumstances; conduct random vehicle checks; monitor vehicles through a control room or travel desk; and provide an optional mobile-app emergency-help function.

These were not merely proposed changes. Karnataka Act No. 38 of 2026 expressly omits clauses (h) through (o). The September 4 Gazette publication also says the amendment came into force at once.

What still applies

The amendment did not delete all of Section 25. The provisions left in place include written willingness from women employees, employer-provided transport from residence to workplace and back at no cost with adequate security, and GPS for tracking and monitoring. The section also continues to address shift rotation, security guards during the night shift, and workplace facilities including separate restrooms and washing facilities. These conditions originated in the 2020 version of Section 25; the 2026 amendment removed only clauses (h) to (o). The 2020 amendment text sets out the original conditions.

There is an ongoing policy dispute about whether draft Karnataka Occupational Safety, Health and Working Conditions Rules preserve enough of the deleted detail. The Karnataka State IT/ITeS Employees Union has argued that draft rules cannot substitute for specific statutory obligations that have been removed. That debate may continue, but it does not prevent employers from retaining sound controls in their own contracts, standard operating procedures and vendor-management processes. The New Indian Express reported the union’s position.

Why this matters beyond legal compliance

Night-shift transport is a chain of handovers: roster to travel desk, travel desk to driver, driver to employee, and an escalation team when the trip changes. The weak points are usually ordinary operational failures: an unverified replacement driver, a changed route that is not communicated, a missed pickup, an address visible to more people than need it, or a rider who cannot reach a responsible person after midnight.

GPS is useful evidence and can support a response. But a safety system needs more: a current trip manifest, a named owner for exceptions, a way to contact the employee, authority to arrange a replacement trip, and a record of whether the issue was resolved. Without those elements, tracking may document a problem after the fact rather than help prevent harm.

This matters especially for women employees because the choice to accept a night shift should not depend on whether they are willing to absorb uncertainty in the journey home. For corporate leaders, it is also a workforce-design issue: transport arrangements influence whether late-shift roles are genuinely accessible to the people the organisation wants to employ and retain.

A practical employer baseline

1. Keep driver and vehicle diligence. Maintain a current record of the assigned driver, vehicle, licence and vendor contacts for every trip. A last-minute substitution should require a documented check and prompt notice to affected riders.

2. Control changes, not just planned routes. Employees should know their pickup window, vehicle and escalation contact before travel. When a route, vehicle or driver changes, the system should say who approves it, who informs the employee and what happens if the employee does not confirm.

3. Protect home and trip data. A night-shift transport programme needs address and contact information, but not everyone needs access to it. Limit access by role, avoid using uncontrolled message groups for sensitive details, and set retention periods that reflect operational need and applicable privacy obligations.

4. Test the exception path. Run a practical exercise for a missed pickup, vehicle breakdown, route deviation and rider discomfort. Ask: who receives the alert at 1 am, how quickly can they arrange an alternative, and how is the employee told that help is on its way?

5. Review service reliability and employee experience. Track late pickups, cancellations, unplanned substitutions, route deviations, complaints and closure times. Review these with the transport vendor and with employees who use the service; a dashboard alone will not reveal whether riders feel able to report a concern.

6. Make non-retaliation explicit. Employees should be able to flag an unsafe or unsuitable arrangement, or decline it, without having to negotiate individually with a manager or driver. Written willingness to work a night shift is not a substitute for a usable transport escalation process.

Where carpooling fits—and where it does not

Voluntary carpooling can help colleagues coordinate some commutes, particularly on similar corridors and outside employer-managed night transport. But it should not be assumed to replace the transport obligation that remains in Section 25 for women working night shifts. Before offering a shared-ride option for such journeys, an employer should obtain appropriate legal advice and test whether the arrangement actually meets its statutory duties and internal safety standards.

If an employer uses TrustRoute or another carpool platform, it should treat the product as one possible coordination layer—not as a safety guarantee or a substitute for governance. The employer should independently verify how identity checks, trip visibility, data access, incident reporting, support availability and escalation responsibilities work in the specific deployment. Clear policies and accountable human response remain essential.

The management question

Karnataka’s amendment removed detailed statutory instructions, but it did not make the night commute an employee’s private problem. The remaining legal conditions still require employer-provided, free transport with adequate security and GPS tracking for women employees working night shifts.

The better leadership question is therefore not, “Which checklist items can we delete?” It is: “If a colleague’s planned journey changes at midnight, do they know who is responsible, how to get help and what will happen next?” If the answer is unclear, the transport programme needs work—regardless of how many GPS dots appear on a screen.
