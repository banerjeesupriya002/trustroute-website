Corporate Mobility · 2026-10-19

Two-wheelers—not cars—are urban workers’ leading commute mode. Employers should plan accordingly.

India’s first National Household Travel Survey finds that two-wheelers are the leading primary mode for urban workers’ work trips, while cars account for a much smaller share. Employers should start with the modes people already use—walking, public transport and two-wheelers—and add verified carpooling where recurring private-car trips and spare seats make it practical.

For many office campuses, the default commute solution is carpooling. The first National Household Travel Survey suggests that this is too narrow a starting point.

Among urban workers aged 15 and above who reported a work trip of more than 1 km on the survey travel day, 52.3% used a two-wheeler as their primary mode. Cars accounted for 5.7%. Walking accounted for 13.5%, buses 9.2%, auto-rickshaws 8.8%, bicycles 5.8%, and train, Metro, monorail and tram together 5.0%.

That does not mean every office commute is made by scooter or motorcycle. The National Household Travel Survey measures the primary mode—the mode used for the longest part of a trip—and its table covers workers travelling to fixed or non-fixed workplaces. It is nevertheless a valuable national signal for employers: a car-only view of commute planning misses most urban workers’ reported primary modes.

The National Statistical Office’s NHTS release reports that the survey, conducted from July 2025 to June 2026, covered 492,023 households and 2,034,371 people. It counted trips of more than 1 km. Its scale makes it useful for setting priorities, but employers should still collect their own aggregated site-level data before deciding what to fund or change.

Start with the commute that exists

An employee arriving on a two-wheeler, after a walk from a bus stop, or through a Metro-plus-last-mile trip does not need a campaign telling them to share a car. They need the trip’s difficult parts to work: a safe crossing, a usable gate, predictable pickup information, a dry waiting area during rain, or parking that is secure and close enough to be practical.

That distinction matters because an office can waste effort trying to manufacture carpool supply where there are few drivers with spare seats. At the same time, it can overlook inexpensive operational improvements that make the dominant modes safer and less frustrating.

The useful question for HR, facilities and workplace-experience teams is not, “How do we get everyone to carpool?” It is: “How do employees actually reach this site, and where does the journey become difficult, unsafe or unreliable?”

A mobility hierarchy for office sites

A practical plan should begin with the modes employees already depend on, then add shared-car options where there is a genuine match.

  1. Walking and waiting: Check the route between gates, nearby transit stops and pickup points. Continuous footpaths, crossings, lighting, shade and clearly marked pickup areas are basic commute infrastructure—not cosmetic extras.
  2. Bus and rail connections: Publish straightforward information on nearby bus, Metro and rail access; align any office shuttle with actual shift times; and identify where a first- or last-mile gap is preventing an otherwise workable public-transport trip. National figures can mask strong local differences: the NHTS reports rail-based modes at 20.7% of urban worker trips in Maharashtra, 16.5% in West Bengal and 14.4% in Delhi, versus 5.0% nationally.
  3. Two-wheelers: Treat two-wheeler users as a core part of the workforce. That means secure, well-lit parking; pedestrian-safe circulation around vehicle bays; clear entry and exit arrangements; and, where feasible, lockers or space for rain gear. Moving two-wheelers to a remote corner of a campus may simply shift inconvenience and personal-security concerns onto the largest commuter group.
  4. Carpooling: Offer it as a voluntary option for people who already drive—or would use a car—on similar routes and schedules. It is most credible where a recurring trip, spare seats and willing participants already exist.

Road safety is part of workplace access

Employers do not control public roads, but their premises can either reduce or add avoidable risk. India recorded 487,707 road crashes and 177,175 road fatalities in 2024, according to a Ministry of Road Transport and Highways reply in the Rajya Sabha. The ministry lists speeding, mobile-phone use, wrong-side or lane-indisciplined driving, red-light jumping, alcohol or drug use, and non-use of helmets and seat belts among the multiple causes of crashes.

For an office, the relevant lesson is operational. A rushed shift handover, a queue spilling onto the carriageway, an unlit pickup bay, or a parking layout that forces pedestrians to mingle with moving vehicles can make the beginning or end of a commute less safe. A site access review should therefore include gate queues, crossings, waiting zones, traffic marshals where needed, and conflict points between pedestrians, two-wheelers, cars and buses.

Measure before selecting an intervention

Employers do not need precise home addresses or continuous tracking to understand commute patterns. A voluntary survey using broad origin clusters or PIN-code areas, usual mode, work timing, parking use and stated barriers can provide a useful baseline. Report results in aggregate and limit access to identifiable data.

For each office and shift, monitor:

  • usual mode share and major commute barriers;
  • arrival and departure peaks, gate queue times and parking occupancy;
  • two-wheeler parking capacity, lighting, circulation and reported incidents;
  • walking distance and conditions between the site and transit or pickup points;
  • overlap between residential corridors, schedules and employees willing to offer or take a carpool seat.

This prevents a generic mobility programme from becoming a parking-management exercise. A campus might find a real carpool opportunity on one corridor with several regular car drivers, while the larger need is better two-wheeler parking and a safer transfer from a Metro or bus stop. Those call for different interventions.

Use carpooling where it fits

A carpool pilot should be voluntary, limited to a defined corridor or commuter cluster, and evaluated against practical outcomes: completed shared trips, cancellations, pickup reliability, parking demand and participant feedback. Employers should not require employees to disclose their home addresses publicly or treat participation as a condition for retaining access to parking.

For employees who want to share a private car already making the commute, structure matters. The takeaway

The NHTS does not support treating the office commute as chiefly a car-sharing problem. Its urban work-trip data puts two-wheelers first, with substantial use of walking, buses, auto-rickshaws and rail-based modes around them.

For employers, the sensible order is to make existing modes safer and easier first, using site-specific evidence rather than national averages alone. Verified carpooling belongs in that system as a targeted, voluntary option for recurring private-car corridors with available seats—not as a universal answer to the Indian office commute.
