A bus strike is also an attendance-policy test for Delhi employers

Delhi’s DTC driver disruption has made one point clear: when a bus link fails, asking employees to “make alternate arrangements” can turn a public-transport problem into an attendance, cost and late-shift safety problem. Employers need fair rules before the next disruption.

A bus strike is easy to describe as a transport problem. For a bus-dependent employee, it can quickly become an attendance problem, a household-cost problem and, for an early or late shift, a safety problem.

Delhi’s current disruption illustrates the gap. Contractual drivers operating Delhi Transport Corporation (DTC) buses have been on strike over pay, benefits and working conditions. Delhi Traffic Police warned on September 17 that movement could be affected and asked people to plan ahead. Reporting on September 18 said more than 10,000 drivers were on strike, with more than 80% of DTC buses off the road, according to union figures. The Delhi Metro Rail Corporation added eight trains for 20 additional trips, while Metro ridership rose from 72.73 lakh passenger journeys on Monday to 80.71 lakh on Wednesday.

Those figures should not be read as proof that Metro can simply replace a missing bus network. They show how quickly pressure shifts when a feeder mode fails. For many commuters, the bus is the link between home and the station, or between a station and an office, hospital, industrial area or shift workplace. A Metro route may cover the longest part of the journey while still leaving an expensive, inaccessible or unsafe final leg.

The workplace question is not “Who is late?”

During a disruption, a blanket instruction to “make alternate arrangements” sounds neutral but is not neutral in practice. One employee may have a nearby Metro station and flexible hours; another may depend on a bus from an outer colony, have a fixed reporting time, or need to get home after public transport has thinned out. The second employee is more likely to pay extra for an auto or cab, take a longer walk, or arrive late despite making a reasonable effort.

That is why employers should treat a verified, citywide transit disruption as a temporary exception to normal attendance rules—not as an individual performance failure. This is particularly important for frontline staff, junior employees, workers with caregiving responsibilities, people with disabilities, and women travelling at hours when a last-mile arrangement may not feel acceptable or safe.

Four decisions to make before the next disruption

1. State the attendance rule early

Once an official advisory or a credible service disruption is confirmed, tell staff what changes for that day: which roles may work remotely, which reporting times are flexible, and how employees should flag a genuine travel constraint. Managers should be specifically told not to impose attendance deductions, lost incentives or informal pressure to hire costly private transport because a usual bus connection has failed.

The key is clarity. Employees should not have to negotiate separately with every supervisor while standing in a queue or trying to find a ride.

2. Separate essential work from a fixed start time

Not every job can be done remotely, but not every on-site role needs every employee to arrive at the same time. Teams can identify the minimum on-site coverage required for safety and operations, then use reporting windows for the remaining staff. A staggered plan can reduce the risk that an employer sends its entire workforce towards the same crowded interchange at the same hour.

This is not a promise that every journey will be easy. It is a practical way to avoid adding employer-created pressure to an already constrained system.

3. Plan the late-shift decision by mid-afternoon

For employees leaving after dark, “we will see later” is not a transport policy. Set a decision time well before the end of the shift. If a disruption remains active, managers should choose among earlier release, rescheduling non-essential work, remote completion where feasible, or an approved return-trip arrangement for employees who must remain on site.

Employees should retain a genuine choice about any shared-ride option. No one should be expected to wait alone at an unfamiliar pickup point or accept a ride they are uncomfortable taking simply to avoid being marked absent.

4. Map the broken connection, not only the office address

Employers often know where their office is, but not how people actually reach it. A simple anonymous pulse check can identify the most exposed corridors: who relies on buses to reach rail, who has no workable rail substitute, which shifts create a late last-mile problem, and where accessibility needs require dedicated support.

That information is more useful than a generic “Metro-first” message. It helps an employer distinguish between a route where Metro is a realistic fallback and one where it merely moves the difficulty from the bus stop to the station exit.

Where voluntary carpooling can fit

Carpooling is not a substitute for a functioning bus system, and it should never be compulsory. It can, however, be one voluntary option for colleagues travelling similar corridors when the missing connection is a first- or last-mile bus.

The employer’s responsibility is to avoid turning that option into an informal obligation. A good process makes the trip details clear, lets participants decline without explanation, and does not present identity checks or ride-sharing tools as a safety guarantee.

TrustRoute can play a limited role here. Its public product information says it uses identity checks and offers route matching, women-only trip filtering, direct UPI payment, and live trip sharing. Those features may help colleagues who independently choose to share a route coordinate with more information than an ad hoc office group provides. They do not replace an employer’s duty to offer flexibility, make timely late-shift decisions, or support employees for whom sharing a ride is not suitable.

A fairer disruption-day message

A useful employer message need not be long: acknowledge the disruption; state the attendance and reporting-time exception; identify the contact for essential on-site teams and accessibility needs; confirm when the late-shift decision will be made; and remind staff that any carpool arrangement is voluntary.

Delhi’s DTC disruption is a reminder that public transport reliability and workplace policy are connected. Employers cannot restore a missing bus route. They can decide whether the burden of that failure is pushed onto the employees least able to absorb it.
