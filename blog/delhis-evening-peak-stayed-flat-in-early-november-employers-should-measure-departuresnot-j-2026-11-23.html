Delhi’s evening peak stayed flat in early November. Employers should measure departures—not just sign-ins.

CSE’s comparison of Delhi traffic on November 2–6 with October 30 found no flattening of the evening peak during the first week of pollution restrictions. It is not proof that office flexibility failed, but it is a useful warning: arrival flexibility does not automatically change when people leave.

A flexible sign-in can make the morning easier without changing the trip home. That is the practical lesson employers should take from a recent Centre for Science and Environment (CSE) analysis of Delhi traffic—not because the analysis proves that office policies failed, but because it shows why a policy announcement is not the same thing as a changed travel pattern.

CSE compared weekday congestion from November 2–6, 2026, with Friday, October 30, 2026, the last working day before the broader early-November restrictions took effect. Its analysis found that the morning peak was 1% slower, the evening peak was unchanged and the evening Planning Time Index—a measure of the buffer travellers need for unreliable journeys—improved only marginally, from 6.36 to 6.16. The report therefore found no flattening of the evening peak in that short period.

That is a citywide traffic finding, not evidence about any individual employer or worker. It cannot tell us how many people worked from home, which journeys were office commutes, or whether employees changed their departure times. Festival travel, freight, school trips, shopping and other restrictions were also in the mix. But it does expose a common weakness in workplace commute policies: organisations often measure attendance and start times, while overlooking when their on-site workforce actually leaves.

Why the dates and policy context matter

Delhi-NCR’s restrictions did not begin as a single, controlled office-hours experiment. CSE marks restrictions as starting on November 1, while the CAQM order dated October 30 required government and private offices in Delhi and specified NCR districts to operate with up to 50% physical attendance from November 5 through November 20, 2026, subject to exceptions for industrial establishments and essential services where the arrangement was not feasible.

That means only the final two weekdays in CSE’s November 2–6 comparison overlapped with that specific 50% office-attendance order. It would be wrong to claim that work from home or staggered hours “did not work.” A more defensible conclusion is narrower: the city’s evening congestion pattern did not visibly shift during the first restriction week, despite measures intended to reduce travel demand.

The revised GRAP schedule also makes an important distinction. It calls on Delhi and NCR governments to stagger public-office timings, encourages unified employee commutes, calls for stronger public transport, and asks people to prefer public transport or pooled vehicles. These are demand-management measures, not merely instructions to arrive at work at different times.

Arrival flexibility can still produce one exit rush

An organisation may allow employees to begin between 9.30 am and 11 am yet still create a concentrated evening departure. Meetings are scheduled late, approvals wait for managers, teams need overlap with clients, and employees may feel that leaving before a supervisor is noticed. The result is familiar: a wider arrival window followed by a narrow release window at lifts, parking exits, shuttle stops, Metro gates and nearby arterial roads.

For Indian employers, this is not only a congestion issue. A delayed exit can mean missed feeder connections, a more expensive last-mile trip, longer exposure at poorly designed pickup points and a less workable journey home after dark. These burdens are not shared evenly: staff with fixed shifts, care responsibilities, long transfers or limited late-evening transport options usually have the least ability to absorb a vague instruction to “leave later.”

Measure the departure curve, with clear limits

The practical response is not surveillance and it is not compulsory carpooling. It is a short, privacy-conscious test of whether workplace schedules are compressing travel demand.

For four weeks, an employer can use aggregated evidence such as parking-exit counts, shuttle boardings, building turnstile totals and an optional commute survey. The organisation should state clearly what is being collected, keep results at group level, avoid using them for performance management and set a deletion date. A badge swipe is an imperfect proxy for the start of a commute; it should not be treated as continuous location tracking.

A useful review asks:

  • Departure distribution: What proportion of on-site staff leaves in each 30-minute block?
  • Pressure points: When do parking exits, shuttle queues and pickup bays become congested?
  • Mode and connection constraints: Which groups depend on Metro, bus, two-wheelers, shuttles or a late last-mile connection?
  • Reliability: Are employees reporting a recurring worst-case journey, rather than only a higher average journey time?
  • Fairness: Do protected departure windows work for shift staff, junior employees, caregivers and people who need a safer after-dark trip home?

Results should be read corridor by corridor. A workplace cannot credibly claim that it has solved Delhi traffic. It can, however, identify whether its own end-of-day routines are adding avoidable pressure to a gate, station approach or shared shuttle route.

Build an exit policy, not just a start-time policy

Once a pattern is visible, employers can test modest changes: meeting-free departure windows, team coverage plans that rotate fairly, earlier handovers for roles that allow them, and shuttle timetables that match actual exit demand. The aim is not to force everyone out early. It is to make more than one departure window genuinely usable.

A team-based approach is usually more realistic than telling every employee to choose any time. For example, teams can agree a protected departure window for a trial period, while maintaining adequate customer or operational coverage. Managers then need to respect it: a protected 5.15 pm exit is meaningless if the recurring status call begins at 5 pm.

There must also be a practical fallback. A later departure can be unsuitable where the last Metro or feeder connection is limited, a pickup point is poorly lit, or staff are travelling after dark. Employers should not shift inconvenience or risk from the road network onto employees simply to improve an internal timetable.

Where voluntary carpooling fits

Public transport, walking, cycling and employer shuttles should remain central to a lower-car commute. Carpooling is most useful in a narrower situation: people already driving similar routes at compatible times, especially where a first-mile, last-mile or shift-time gap makes other options difficult.

Predictable departure bands can make that coordination easier, but participation should remain voluntary. Employers should not require employees to disclose their home address to colleagues, accept a ride they are uncomfortable with or use a shared car as a substitute for a late-shift transport and escalation plan.

The employer takeaway

CSE’s early-November comparison does not establish why Delhi’s evening peak stayed flat. It does establish that broad restrictions did not produce an obvious citywide evening-peak shift over those five weekdays. Employers should treat that as a prompt to check their own evidence.

If flexibility changes only sign-in time, the evening commute may remain exactly where it was. Measuring aggregated departures, protecting workable exit windows and keeping options voluntary can make a workplace commute policy more credible—for people who use Metro, buses, shuttles, two-wheelers, walk or choose to share a car.
