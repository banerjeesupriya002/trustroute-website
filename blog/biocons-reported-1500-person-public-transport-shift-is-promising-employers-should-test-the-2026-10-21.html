Corporate Mobility · 2026-10-21

Biocon’s reported 1,500-person public-transport shift is promising. Employers should test the whole commute before copying it.

Biocon’s Bengaluru programme offers a useful corporate-mobility lesson: incentives work better when employees can plan and complete the full door-to-door journey. But the reported results should be treated as an early case study—not a template to impose on every workforce or shift.

A transit incentive is useful only if an employee can complete the trip around it.

Biocon’s Bengaluru corporate-commute programme is a worthwhile case study for employers—but not because it proves that every company can quickly move large numbers of people from private vehicles to public transport. The more useful lesson is narrower: a Metro or bus benefit needs to be paired with practical help for the first mile, the last mile and the working hours in between.

On October 9, 2026, The Times of India reported, citing data shared by WRI India, that more than 1,500 Biocon employees had switched to public transport within four months of the programme starting in April. The report also said the number of cab users fell from 1,396 to 858, a 39% reduction. Those are encouraging programme figures, but they should not be read as independently audited evidence of permanent behaviour change or as a guarantee that the same result will occur at another site.

What the programme got right

Biocon’s initiative sits within the Station Access and Mobility Program (STAMP), led by WRI India and the Toyota Mobility Foundation with support from Bengaluru transport and industry bodies. The partners describe it as a corporate nudge programme using a customised mobility-as-a-service interface through Tummoc, alongside behavioural prompts, incentives and attention to first- and last-mile access.

That design matters. Public transport is rarely rejected simply because an employee dislikes the train or bus itself. The failure point may be a difficult walk to the stop, an uncertain feeder connection, an expensive final auto ride, a transfer that does not fit a shift change, or the absence of a viable option after normal services reduce.

WRI’s wider STAMP work identifies this access problem directly: gaps between Metro stations and the rest of a journey can depress use even where rail infrastructure exists. Its pilots have included solutions ranging from electric autorickshaws to carpooling applications. The point is not that every gap needs a car. It is that the main transit leg succeeds only when the surrounding links work.

Why copying only the incentive would miss the point

An employer could reimburse Metro tickets and still see little change if the commute remains unreliable or unsuitable for large parts of the workforce. This is especially relevant in Indian employment hubs where staff travel across long corridors, work staggered shifts or finish after frequent public transport has thinned out.

For employers, the better question is not, “How many people claimed a pass?” It is, “Which part of the home-to-work journey prevents a workable lower-car trip?” The answer will differ by office location, shift, gender, household responsibilities and access to buses, Metro, walking routes and two-wheelers.

A practical way to test the model

1. Establish a voluntary, privacy-conscious baseline. Survey broad origin areas, usual modes, shift windows, travel-time reliability and the reasons employees do not use public transport. Do not collect precise home addresses unless there is a clear need and a documented privacy basis. Aggregated clusters are usually enough to identify recurring gaps.

2. Separate the main transit leg from the access problem. Identify whether the barrier is route information, a safe walking connection, feeder frequency, a final-kilometre cost, a late-shift gap or a poor campus pickup arrangement. A single transport benefit will not solve every one of these problems.

3. Pilot by corridor and shift. Start with a limited group of employees who share a realistic route or timing pattern. Test route guidance, feeder support, secure pickup points or a shift-specific connector. Keep participation voluntary and retain fair alternatives for people without a practical public-transport option.

4. Measure repeat use, not just sign-ups. Track whether employees continue using the option after the first few weeks, whether their journey time becomes more predictable, and where trips fail. A rise in registrations without repeat use may indicate that the incentive is attractive but the journey is still not workable.

5. Treat women’s experience and late-shift travel as core design inputs. Ask separately about walking, waiting, transfers, pickup visibility and confidence in the available options. A programme that works at 8:30 am may not be acceptable at 10:30 pm. Employees should never be pressured into shared travel or public transport where they do not consider it suitable.

Where voluntary carpooling can help—and where it cannot

Carpooling is not a substitute for frequent buses, Metro capacity, safe footpaths or an employer’s duty to plan late-shift transport responsibly. It can, however, be a limited complement where a repeated gap remains: colleagues travelling from the same housing cluster, a first-mile link to a station, or a corridor with no credible transit option at a particular time.

The governance standard matters. Employers should avoid informal spreadsheets that expose phone numbers and personal routines. Any voluntary arrangement should make clear that participation is optional, cost-sharing is transparent, personal data is minimised, and employees have a way to report concerns or opt out.

The employer lesson

Biocon’s reported outcome is encouraging because it tests a more complete proposition than “buy a pass and hope.” But the transferable lesson is not to chase one headline number. It is to design around the actual commute chain, test solutions with employees, protect choice and measure where the system still fails.

For Indian employers, public transport should usually remain the backbone where it is available. The corporate role is to make that backbone usable in real life—then offer carefully governed, voluntary options for the journeys that still fall through the gaps.
