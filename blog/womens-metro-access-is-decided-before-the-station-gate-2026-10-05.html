Women’s Metro access is decided before the station gate

A new Hyderabad study finds that perceived safety and security are associated with first- and last-mile mode choices, including greater use of personal cars by women on some commute legs. The policy lesson is not to replace Metro with cars: it is to make walking, waiting and feeder connections safer—and to treat verified cost-sharing as an optional bridge where a car is still needed.

A Metro line can be fast, frequent and air-conditioned, yet still fail a commuter before she reaches the station. The first decision of the day may be whether to walk along a dark stretch, wait for an uncertain feeder, take an auto-rickshaw, ask family for a lift—or drive.

That is why station access is a women’s mobility issue, not merely a question of how many metres separate a home or office from a Metro gate.

A Transport Policy study published in October 2026 surveyed 4,282 transit commuters at seven Hyderabad Metro stations and modelled first- and last-mile choices separately by trip direction. It found that perceived safety and security had a positive association with choosing a personal car and a negative association with choosing a personal two-wheeler across the models. Women were more inclined to choose a personal car for home-based first-mile trips and workplace-bound last-mile trips; for home-bound last-mile trips, they were more inclined to choose an auto-rickshaw. Read the study.

The finding needs careful interpretation. This was a survey-based mode-choice study, not evidence that a single unsafe street causes somebody to drive, nor evidence that every woman can access a car. It also does not show that carpooling is the preferred answer. What it does show is that safety and security perceptions belong in the practical calculation of how people connect to rail.

A station catchment is not the same as usable access

Planning discussions often use a station’s walking radius as shorthand for accessibility. But a 500-metre walk is not equally usable at 8 am, after a late shift or in heavy rain. It can include a missing footpath, a hostile crossing, poor lighting, a long wait at a feeder stop or a pickup point where someone is isolated.

For many Indian women, the journey can also involve off-peak travel, several linked stops and care responsibilities. The World Bank’s India-focused gender toolkit calls for measures beyond the vehicle itself: well-placed lighting, better walking and cycling infrastructure, easier transfers, services at times women need them and effective grievance systems. See the World Bank’s guidance.

The important analytical distinction is this: proximity is a map measure; access is an experience measure. A household car used to reach a station may therefore be filling a feeder gap. That is an inference about the travel chain, not a reason to treat more private-car use as the transport goal.

Do not solve a feeder problem by abandoning public transport

Individual car trips may remove uncertainty for people who can use them, but they also add congestion, parking demand and exclusion for commuters without a car. The durable solution remains public: continuous footpaths, safe crossings, lighting, visible station approaches, reliable feeder services, orderly auto-rickshaw access and a credible way to report failures.

This is consistent with the Economic Survey 2025–26’s argument that streets should be treated as social infrastructure rather than only as traffic corridors, with safety, comfort and accessibility built into street design. Read the Economic Survey chapter on urbanisation.

However, infrastructure improvements take time. Some employees live beyond a practical walking distance, work irregular shifts or travel through areas where a feeder link is unavailable. In those cases, a shared ride can be a limited, voluntary connector to Metro—not a replacement for a bus, a footpath or the train journey itself.

What a safer shared-link option should—and should not—do

Informal office and neighbourhood carpools can be useful, but a group-chat arrangement can leave a rider to assess identity, vehicle, fare, pickup location and reliability at short notice. Verification and clearer trip information can reduce some of that uncertainty. They cannot establish that a ride is safe, predict driving behaviour or replace personal judgement, emergency support and safer streets.

For a regular station-feeder carpool, commuters should be able to see basic information before deciding: who is offering the ride, the pickup point, the stated fare, the route or destination, and a record that helps indicate whether plans are regularly honoured. A well-lit, agreed pickup point is generally preferable to an improvised isolated corner. Women-only matching can be a useful choice for some commuters, but it should be voluntary rather than presented as a substitute for fixing public space.

A practical checklist for employers and Metro agencies

Employers near Metro corridors can help without pretending to solve the city’s transport system:

  • Ask where the trip breaks. Separate home-to-station and station-to-office questions, and review answers by shift timing and gender. A nearby station may not be usable for a particular shift.
  • Improve the immediate handoff. Where feasible, coordinate with building management and local agencies on lighting, crossings, signage and visible pickup or drop-off areas near the workplace.
  • Keep participation voluntary. Offer information about public, shared and employer-supported options, but do not require location histories or pressure staff to carpool.
  • Measure reliability as well as uptake. Track reported missed pickups, long waits, feeder gaps and recurring trouble spots. A nominal mobility option that repeatedly fails at departure time is not dependable access.

Metro agencies and city planners should add experience-based measures to their station-access reviews: comfort by time of day, crossing quality, lighting, feeder wait times, auto-rickshaw availability and the share of passengers being dropped off or driving. The Hyderabad study’s separation of first-mile and last-mile trips is useful here because the barriers may differ when someone is leaving home, leaving work or returning after dark.

The takeaway: Metro inclusion starts outside the station. Safer walking and dependable feeder public transport are the foundation. Where a car remains necessary for part of the trip, a transparent, voluntary and verified cost-sharing arrangement can be one additional option—but never a substitute for the public conditions that let women travel independently.
